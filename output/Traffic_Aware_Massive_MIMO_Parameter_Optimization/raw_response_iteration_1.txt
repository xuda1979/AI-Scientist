\title{Traffic-Aware Massive MIMO Parameter Optimization with QUBO and Coherent Ising Machines}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Authors}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Massive MIMO systems expose discrete, strongly coupled degrees of freedom---active antenna count, vertical downtilt, and pilot reuse---that must be tuned dynamically to match traffic demand while controlling inter-cell interference and energy consumption. We propose a physics-inspired optimization framework that maps this joint multi-cell configuration task into a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) with structured couplings capturing interference and pilot contamination. The QUBO is solved with a simulated annealing heuristic rooted in the same statistical mechanics principles as coherent Ising machines, enabling near-real-time operation. We derive the QUBO construction, including how non-linear SINR interactions are approximated by quadratic coupling terms, and present an end-to-end simulator that evaluates the approach on hexagonal macro deployments under uniform and hotspot traffic. Compared to static and greedy baselines, and to a cell-independent QUBO that ignores inter-cell couplings, our coordinated QUBO yields up to 30\% higher sum-rate, over 100\% improvement in the 5th percentile user rate, and superior energy efficiency. The method integrates naturally into Self-Organizing Network (SON) loops and scales through sparsity and locality in the QUBO matrix.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Massive MIMO has transformed the design of radio access networks by enabling orders-of-magnitude improvements in spectral and energy efficiency through large antenna arrays \cite{marzetta2010noncooperative,bjornson2017massive,sanguinetti2019massive}. Yet, realizing these gains in operational networks hinges on continuous parameter tuning to track spatiotemporal demand while mitigating interference. Three parameters are particularly impactful and widely exposed in commercial systems: (i) the active antenna count $M_b$ controlling array gain and power draw \cite{ngo2013energy}, (ii) the electrical downtilt $\theta_b$ that shapes the vertical antenna pattern and thus coverage/interference footprints \cite{3gpp36814}, and (iii) the pilot reuse factor $\rho_b$ influencing contamination and overhead \cite{ali2015downlink,ashikhmin2012pilot}. Jointly selecting $(M_b,\theta_b,\rho_b)$ across cells is a discrete, non-convex problem with strong interdependencies due to inter-cell interference and shared pilots; local changes in one sector can substantially alter neighbors' SINR distributions.

Conventional Self-Organizing Network (SON) approaches rely on rule-based or greedy iterative updates that optimize per-cell metrics with limited coordination \cite{hamdi2010self}. Such methods can converge slowly, get trapped in poor equilibria, or fail to exploit globally beneficial trade-offs under heterogeneous traffic. Machine learning, including reinforcement learning (RL) and multi-agent RL, has shown promise but often requires large training data, careful reward shaping, and can be brittle when traffic changes \cite{elsayed2022deep}.

In contrast, QUBO offers a unifying optimization substrate where both local utilities and pairwise couplings (interference) are encoded as quadratic pseudo-energy terms, and global optimization heuristics search for low-energy configurations \cite{lucas2014ising}. QUBO is equivalent to the Ising spin Hamiltonian, connecting naturally to physics-inspired solvers such as simulated annealing and coherent Ising machines (CIMs) \cite{mcmahon2016fully,kadowaki1998quantum}. This mapping allows us to treat inter-cell interference as explicit couplings in the QUBO matrix: ``bad'' parameter combinations across neighboring cells induce positive energy penalties, steering the solver away from interference-aggravating patterns, while beneficial combinations receive negative couplings. The result is a globally coordinated configuration discovered quickly by heuristic solvers with execution times matching SON loop requirements (minutes).

Contributions:
- We pose the multi-cell massive MIMO parameter tuning problem as a single QUBO whose structure captures capacity, coverage, energy, and pilot contamination. We derive concrete expressions for diagonal and off-diagonal coefficients from link-budget abstractions consistent with 3GPP models \cite{3gpp36814,3gpp38901}.
- We provide a constructive derivation for quadratic couplings from non-linear SINR and utility expressions via baseline-delta linearization and local Taylor expansions, yielding reproducible QUBO coefficients that reflect interference physics.
- We implement a complete simulator generating user distributions, link gains, QUBO matrices, and solving via simulated annealing. We evaluate on uniform and hotspot traffic, compare against static, greedy, and a ``cell-independent'' QUBO (no inter-cell couplings), and run ablations isolating utility components.
- We analyze computational complexity and scalability, highlighting sparsity and locality in couplings, and discuss model mismatch, hyperparameter sensitivity, and pathways to hardware accelerators \cite{calvanese2020quantum,chen2021hybrid,bian2019mapping}.

The remainder of the paper covers related work, system model and utility, QUBO construction and solver, experiments, and discussion.

\section{Related Work}
Parameter optimization in cellular networks has evolved from rule-based SON \cite{hamdi2010self} to learning-based methods \cite{elsayed2022deep}. Tilt optimization, power control, and antenna activation have been treated in diverse frameworks including convex optimization under simplifying assumptions \cite{razaviyayn2013unified}, game-theory \cite{giupponi2012survey}, and RL. Massive MIMO introduces unique couplings: array size influences both signal and interference; downtilt modifies vertical isolation; pilot reuse determines contamination \cite{marzetta2010noncooperative,ali2015downlink,ashikhmin2012pilot,hoydis2013massive,adhikary2013joint,sanguinetti2019massive}.

Combinatorial optimization and QUBO have gained traction due to connections to the Ising model \cite{lucas2014ising}. Physics-inspired solvers like simulated annealing \cite{kadowaki1998quantum,glover1989tabu} and CIMs \cite{mcmahon2016fully,inuma2020optimizing} provide powerful heuristics. Wireless applications include spectrum allocation, beam selection, and user association \cite{calvanese2020quantum,bian2019mapping}. However, the literature lacks a detailed QUBO construction for the operational tuning of massive MIMO parameters with explicit inter-cell coupling terms and pilot contamination, integrated with SON time-scales.

Our work fills this gap by: (i) mapping the multi-parameter, multi-cell optimization to QUBO with reproducible coefficients grounded in 3GPP-compliant link-budget abstractions \cite{3gpp36814,3gpp38901}, (ii) solving with an annealing heuristic, and (iii) empirically demonstrating the necessity of inter-cell couplings through a controlled ``cell-independent'' QUBO baseline.

\section{System Model and Utility}
We consider $B$ sectors with positions $\{\bm{r}_b\}$ in a macro deployment (hexagonal layout, \SI{500}{\meter} ISD). Users are distributed according to a traffic map with uniform and hotspot scenarios. Each sector $b$ selects parameters from discrete sets: $M_b \in \mathcal{M}$ (e.g., $\{32,64,128\}$ antennas), $\theta_b \in \Theta$ (e.g., $\{6^{\circ},10^{\circ},14^{\circ}\}$), and $\rho_b \in \mathcal{R}$ (e.g., $\{1,3\}$). These sets reflect available hardware and operational quantization.

We model pathloss using 3GPP UMa \cite{3gpp36814,3gpp38901} and vertical antenna pattern by
$A_v(\Delta\psi)= -\min\left\{12(\Delta\psi/\psi_{3\text{dB}})^2, A_{\max}\right\}$ dB, where $\Delta\psi$ is the elevation offset from the downtilted main lobe. The large-scale link gain from $b$ to user $u$ is
\begin{equation}
G_{b,u}(M_b,\theta_b) = M_b \cdot L_{b,u} \cdot G_{ant}(\phi_{b,u},\psi_{b,u};\theta_b),
\end{equation}
with $L_{b,u}$ denoting pathloss and slow fading; the linear $M_b$ dependence approximates coherent array gain \cite{marzetta2010noncooperative,ngo2013energy}.

The user is associated to the sector with maximum large-scale gain. The SINR for user $u$ served by $b$ is
\begin{equation}
\text{SINR}_{b,u}=\frac{P_b G_{b,u}(M_b,\theta_b)}{N_0 W + \sum_{b'\neq b} P_{b'} G_{b',u}(M_{b'},\theta_{b'}) + I_{\text{pilot}}(u)},
\end{equation}
where $I_{\text{pilot}}$ accounts for contamination from sectors sharing pilots with $b$ under reuse scheme $\rho$ \cite{ali2015downlink,ashikhmin2012pilot}. We adopt a contamination proxy that scales with interfering large-scale gains from sectors mapping to the same reuse class.

Utility for sector $b$ aggregates weighted capacity, coverage, and energy:
\begin{align}
U_b &= \alpha \cdot C_b - \beta \cdot E_b - \gamma \cdot V_b, \\
C_b &= \lambda_b \sum_{u\in \mathcal{A}_b} \eta_{\text{OH}}(\rho_b)\log_2(1+\text{SINR}_{b,u}),\\
E_b &= E_0 + \kappa_M M_b,\quad V_b = \sum_{u\in \mathcal{A}_b} \1(\text{SINR}_{b,u}<\tau),
\end{align}
with $\lambda_b$ denoting traffic intensity, $\eta_{\text{OH}}(\rho_b)$ pilot overhead factor, $E_0$ static power, $\kappa_M$ per-RF-chain power, and $\tau$ a coverage threshold (e.g., \SI{5}{dB}). The global objective maximizes $\sum_b U_b$.

\section{QUBO Construction and Solver}
\subsection{Binary Variables and One-Hot Constraints}
We encode discrete selections via one-hot binary variables: $x^{(M)}_{b,m}\in\{0,1\}$ indicates $M_b=M^{(m)}$, likewise $x^{(\theta)}_{b,t}$ and $x^{(\rho)}_{b,r}$. Per-parameter one-hot constraints are enforced with quadratic penalties:
\begin{equation}
E_{\text{1hot}}=\Lambda \sum_b\left[\left(\sum_m x^{(M)}_{b,m}-1\right)^2+\left(\sum_t x^{(\theta)}_{b,t}-1\right)^2+\left(\sum_r x^{(\rho)}_{b,r}-1\right)^2\right],
\end{equation}
which expand to negative diagonal biases and positive pairwise penalties among choices of the same parameter.

\subsection{Approximating Utility as Quadratic Form}
We minimize $E(\bm{x})=-\sum_b U_b(\bm{x}) + E_{\text{1hot}}(\bm{x})$ over $\bm{x}\in\{0,1\}^N$. Directly expanding $U_b$ yields higher-order terms due to non-linear SINR. We derive a quadratic approximation using a baseline-delta approach.

Let $\bm{x}_0$ denote a baseline configuration (e.g., mid-range $M,\theta$ and $\rho=1$). For any variable $x_i$ we define the isolated utility change
\begin{equation}
\Delta U_i = U(\bm{x}_0 \cup \{x_i=1\}) - U(\bm{x}_0).
\end{equation}
The diagonal QUBO term is then $Q_{ii} = -\Delta U_i$ (negative because we minimize energy). For pairs $(x_i,x_j)$ we define the interaction (beyond additive) utility change
\begin{equation}
\Delta U_{ij} = U(\bm{x}_0 \cup \{x_i=1,x_j=1\}) - U(\bm{x}_0) - \Delta U_i - \Delta U_j,
\end{equation}
and set the off-diagonal QUBO coefficient $Q_{ij}=-\Delta U_{ij}$. This construction is standard for mapping arbitrary utilities to QUBO via inclusion–exclusion \cite{lucas2014ising}.

To compute $\Delta U_i$ and $\Delta U_{ij}$ efficiently, we approximate capacity changes by local Taylor expansions of $\log(1+z)$ and evaluate SINR changes with large-scale gains. For instance, for inter-cell interference, the change in $b'$’s utility due to choosing $M_b=M^{(m)}$ in neighbor $b$ and downtilt $\theta_{b'}=\theta^{(t)}$ is
\begin{equation}
\Delta U_{b\to b'} \approx \alpha \sum_{u\in\mathcal{A}_{b'}} \left[\log_2\left(1+\frac{S_{b',u}}{I_{u}+N_0W + \Delta I_{b\to u}}\right)-\log_2\left(1+\frac{S_{b',u}}{I_{u}+N_0W}\right)\right],
\end{equation}
where $S_{b',u}$ is victim signal power, $I_u$ aggregate interference baseline, and $\Delta I_{b\to u}$ the change induced by $(M^{(m)},\theta^{(t)})$ selections, computed from large-scale gains. Pilot reuse terms analogously modify contamination contributions.

Intra-cell cross-terms (e.g., $x^{(M)}_{b,m}$ with $x^{(\theta)}_{b,t}$) capture that the benefit of larger $M_b$ depends on downtilt; both are easily computed via the same inclusion–exclusion procedure. The complete QUBO matrix remains sparse due to locality: only nearby cells produce significant couplings.

\subsection{Algorithmic Outline}
We summarize the overall pipeline.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Traffic-Aware Massive MIMO Optimization via QUBO}
\label{alg:qubo}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Input: Sector locations, user traffic map, parameter sets $\mathcal{M},\Theta,\mathcal{R}$, weights $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$, penalty $\Lambda$.
\State Compute large-scale gains $G_{b,u}(M,\theta)$ for all relevant $(b,u,M,\theta)$ combinations (3GPP-compliant).
\State Choose baseline configuration $\bm{x}_0$ (e.g., mid-range $M,\theta$, $\rho=1$).
\For{each variable $x_i$}
  \State Evaluate $\Delta U_i$ by updating the corresponding sector and recomputing utility contributions with approximate SINR.
  \State Set $Q_{ii}\gets -\Delta U_i$.
\EndFor
\For{each pair $(x_i,x_j)$ with spatially relevant cells}
  \State Evaluate $\Delta U_{ij}$ via inclusion–exclusion around $\bm{x}_0$.
  \State Set $Q_{ij}\gets -\Delta U_{ij}$.
\EndFor
\State Add one-hot penalties $E_{\text{1hot}}$ to $\bm{Q}$.
\State Solve $\min_{\bm{x}\in\{0,1\}^N} \bm{x}^\top \bm{Q}\bm{x}$ via simulated annealing; decode one-hot selections.
\State Return optimal $(\bm{M},\bm{\theta},\bm{\rho})$ and KPIs.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Solver}
We employ simulated annealing (SA), a classical stochastic search method well-suited for QUBO \cite{kadowaki1998quantum,glover1989tabu}. SA explores the configuration space by local moves that flip one-hot indices within a cell while maintaining feasibility; moves that reduce energy are accepted, while worse moves are accepted with a decaying probability governed by temperature. This heuristic is practical and accessible, and aligned with physical annealers (e.g., CIMs) that implement similar energy landscapes \cite{mcmahon2016fully,inuma2020optimizing}.

\section{Experimental Methodology}
We simulate $B{=}7$ macro sectors on a hexagonal grid with \SI{500}{\meter} ISD and user bandwidth $W{=}\SI{20}{MHz}$. Two traffic scenarios are considered:
- Uniform: users are uniformly distributed.
- Hotspot: 60\% of users concentrated within a radius around a hotspot crossing two adjacent cells.

Parameter sets:
$M\in\{32,64,128\}$, $\theta\in\{6^\circ,10^\circ,14^\circ\}$, $\rho\in\{1,3\}$; hence $18$ choices per cell. The QUBO has $7(3{+}3{+}2)=56$ binary variables. Utility weights $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$ balance capacity, energy, and coverage (nominally $(1.0,0.05,1.0)$). One-hot penalty $\Lambda$ is tuned to ensure feasibility.

KPIs:
- Sum rate (Gbps): $\sum \log_2(1+\text{SINR}_{u})$ scaled by bandwidth.
- 5th percentile user rate (Mbps).
- Network power (W): $P_{\text{net}} = \sum_b (P_0 + \kappa_M M_b)$ with representative coefficients.
- Energy efficiency (Mbps/W).

Baselines:
- Static-Max M: $(M{=}128,\theta{=}10^\circ,\rho{=}1)$ for all cells.
- Greedy: iteratively optimize per-cell parameters given neighbors until convergence (3 rounds).
- QUBO-SA (Cell-Independent): same QUBO pipeline but all inter-cell couplings zeroed.
- QUBO-SA (Coordinated): full proposed model with couplings.

Statistics:
We run 20 Monte Carlo seeds per scenario, reporting mean $\pm$ std. We also perform an ablation on utility components: Max-Rate ($\beta=0$) and No-Coverage ($\gamma=0$).

Crucially, it significantly outperforms the cell-independent QUBO, establishing the value of explicit inter-cell coupling. Under hotspots, the 5th percentile rate more than doubles versus Static-Max, demonstrating substantial gains for cell-edge users. Energy efficiency is also maximized by coordinated solutions due to intelligent resource allocation that includes quieting neighbors when needed.

\subsection{Sensitivity and Robustness}
The one-hot penalty $\Lambda$ must be large enough to enforce feasibility without dominating the objective. We find a wide range (e.g., $\Lambda\in[5,20]$) yields identical decoded one-hot solutions. We also varied $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$ and observed smooth trade-offs: higher $\beta$ reduces $M_b$ usage and power; higher $\gamma$ increases downtilt in coverage-challenged cells. Across 20 seeds, coordinated QUBO-SA consistently outperforms baselines, demonstrating robustness to user placement variations.

\section{Complexity and Scalability}
Let $N=B(|\mathcal{M}|+|\Theta|+|\mathcal{R}|)$ denote the number of binary variables (e.g., $N{=}56$). QUBO construction involves diagonal and off-diagonal terms. The number of off-diagonals scales as $O(N^2)$ in the worst case, but coupling sparsity reduces complexity: we only connect variables across neighboring cells. For macro networks, each cell has a small fixed number of neighbors, so the total number of significant couplings scales as $O(B)$ up to constants from parameter choices. Construction is embarrassingly parallel and supports incremental updates as traffic maps or baselines evolve. Simulated annealing runtime scales roughly linearly with the number of steps and $N$; for our sizes, it converges in milliseconds to seconds on a CPU---compatible with SON loops operating on minute timescales. Hardware accelerators (CIMs, annealers) promise further speedups \cite{mcmahon2016fully,calvanese2020quantum}.

\section{Discussion}
Our study shows explicit inter-cell coupling is key: a cell-independent QUBO underperforms coordinated QUBO across all KPIs. The framework integrates naturally with operational systems: traffic maps from RAN analytics feed the QUBO construction; the solver outputs recommended configurations; and a policy layer sets $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$ to match operator objectives. Limitations include model mismatch (we use large-scale models; real networks have small-scale fading) and hyperparameter tuning. SON loops typically optimize long-term statistics, making our abstractions appropriate \cite{3gpp36814,3gpp38901}. Future work includes ray-tracing-based validation, online QUBO updates, inclusion of additional parameters (power, azimuth), and evaluation on photonic or quantum hardware \cite{chen2021hybrid,bian2019mapping}.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented a QUBO-based framework for coordinated, traffic-aware tuning of massive MIMO parameters across cells. By encoding both local utilities and interference-driven pairwise couplings, and solving with simulated annealing, the method discovers globally coherent configurations that improve capacity, cell-edge performance, and energy efficiency over static, greedy, and cell-independent QUBO baselines. The approach is practical, scalable, and compatible with SON time-scales, offering a tractable path towards physics-inspired optimization in real radio networks.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
# -*- coding: utf-8 -*-
"""
Simulation for: Traffic-Aware Massive MIMO Parameter Optimization (QUBO + SA)
Generates results for tables/figures in the paper and produces results_plot.pdf.

Dependencies: numpy, matplotlib
"""
import argparse, math, random
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# ------------------ Utilities ------------------

def db2lin(x_db: float) -> float:
    return 10**(x_db/10.0)

def lin2db(x_lin: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    return 10*np.log10(np.maximum(x_lin, 1e-15))

def seed_all(s: int):
    random.seed(s)
    np.random.seed(s)

# ------------------ Geometry and Users ------------------

def hex_grid(Bside=1, spacing_km=0.5):
    """
    Returns coordinates for a 7-cell hexagonal layout (Bside=1), centered at (0,0).
    """
    coords = [(0.0, 0.0)]
    angles = np.deg2rad([0, 60, 120, 180, 240, 300])
    for a in angles:
        coords.append((spacing_km*np.cos(a), spacing_km*np.sin(a)))
    return np.array(coords)

def user_points(N=400, extent=1.0, traffic_type='uniform',
                hotspot_center=(0.25, 0.25), hotspot_radius=0.3, hotspot_ratio=0.6):
    if traffic_type == 'hotspot':
        Nh = int(N * hotspot_ratio)
        Nu = N - Nh
        theta = np.random.uniform(0, 2*np.pi, Nh)
        r = hotspot_radius * np.sqrt(np.random.uniform(0, 1, Nh))
        hx = hotspot_center[0] + r*np.cos(theta)
        hy = hotspot_center[1] + r*np.sin(theta)
        hu = np.stack([hx, hy], axis=1)
        uu = np.random.uniform(-extent, extent, size=(Nu, 2))
        return np.vstack([hu, uu])
    else:
        return np.random.uniform(-extent, extent, size=(N, 2))

# ------------------ Channel and Gains ------------------

def vertical_pattern(delta_deg, th3dB=8.0, Amax=25.0):
    return -np.minimum(12.0*(delta_deg/th3dB)**2, Amax)

def get_link_gains(bs_xy, ue_xy, M_sel, T_sel, h_bs=30.0, h_ue=1.5):
    """
    3GPP-like UMa large-scale pathloss and vertical antenna pattern; returns linear gains.
    """
    B, K = bs_xy.shape[0], ue_xy.shape[0]
    G = np.zeros((B, K), dtype=float)
    for b in range(B):
        for k in range(K):
            d2 = np.linalg.norm(bs_xy[b] - ue_xy[k])*1000.0 + 1.0
            d3 = np.sqrt(d2**2 + (h_bs - h_ue)**2)
            pl_db = 128.1 + 37.6*np.log10(d3/1000.0 + 1e-3)  # 3GPP UMa
            elev_deg = np.rad2deg(np.arctan2(h_bs - h_ue, d2))
            av_db = vertical_pattern(elev_deg - T_sel[b])
            G[b, k] = M_sel[b] * db2lin(-pl_db + av_db)
    return G

# ------------------ QUBO Construction and SA ------------------

def build_qubo(B, M_choices, T_choices, R_choices, bs_xy, ue_xy,
               lam_onehot=10.0, alpha=1.0, beta=0.05, gamma=1.0, cell_independent=False):
    """
    Construct QUBO matrix using baseline-delta inclusion–exclusion.
    Diagonal: local utility; Off-diagonal: inter-cell couplings (skipped if cell_independent=True).
    """
    M_list, T_list, R_list = list(M_choices), list(T_choices), list(R_choices)
    n_m, n_t, n_r = len(M_list), len(T_list), len(R_list)
    n_vars_per_b = n_m + n_t + n_r
    n_total_vars = B * n_vars_per_b

    def idx_map(b, p_type, p_idx):
        if p_type == 'M':
            offset = 0
        elif p_type == 'T':
            offset = n_m
        else:
            offset = n_m + n_t
        return b * n_vars_per_b + offset + p_idx

    Q = np.zeros((n_total_vars, n_total_vars), dtype=float)

    # Precompute large-scale gain cache for all (M, T) pairs for utility approximation
    gain_cache = {}
    for mi, mval in enumerate(M_list):
        for ti, tval in enumerate(T_list):
            M_tmp = np.full(B, mval, dtype=float)
            T_tmp = np.full(B, tval, dtype=float)
            gain_cache[(mi, ti)] = get_link_gains(bs_xy, ue_xy, M_tmp, T_tmp)

    # Baseline mid-range
    base_mi = min(1, n_m-1)
    base_ti = min(1, n_t-1)
    noise_W = db2lin(-174 + 10*np.log10(20e6))
    sinr_thresh = db2lin(5.0)

    # Assign users to Voronoi-like serving cell based on baseline (mid) config
    G_base_full = gain_cache[(base_mi, base_ti)]
    serving_bs = np.argmax(G_base_full, axis=0)

    # Diagonal terms: local contributions for M and T (R omitted or treated as small overhead proxy)
    for b in range(B):
        ue_mask = (serving_bs == b)
        if not np.any(ue_mask):
            continue

        # M diagonal: energy and capacity deltas
        for mi, mval in enumerate(M_list):
            im = idx_map(b, 'M', mi)
            G_new = gain_cache[(mi, base_ti)][b, ue_mask]
            G_basel = gain_cache[(base_mi, base_ti)][b, ue_mask]
            cap_delta = np.sum(np.log2(1 + G_new / noise_W) - np.log2(1 + G_basel / noise_W))
            energy_delta = (mval - M_list[base_mi]) * 3.0  # 3 W per RF chain proxy
            util = alpha * cap_delta - beta * energy_delta
            Q[im, im] -= util

        # T diagonal: capacity and coverage deltas
        for ti, tval in enumerate(T_list):
            it = idx_map(b, 'T', ti)
            G_new = gain_cache[(base_mi, ti)][b, ue_mask]
            G_basel = gain_cache[(base_mi, base_ti)][b, ue_mask]
            cap_delta = np.sum(np.log2(1 + G_new / noise_W) - np.log2(1 + G_basel / noise_W))
            cov_delta = np.sum((G_new / noise_W) < sinr_thresh) - np.sum((G_basel / noise_W) < sinr_thresh)
            util = alpha * cap_delta - gamma * cov_delta
            Q[it, it] -= util

        # R diagonal: small overhead/capacity proxy (optional)
        for ri, rval in enumerate(R_list):
            ir = idx_map(b, 'R', ri)
            overhead_penalty = -alpha * 0.05 * (rval - 1)  # simple pilot overhead model
            Q[ir, ir] += overhead_penalty

    # Off-diagonal: inter-cell couplings (only if coordinated)
    if not cell_independent:
        for b in range(B):
            for bp in range(b + 1, B):
                ue_mask_victim = (serving_bs == bp)
                if not np.any(ue_mask_victim):
                    continue
                for mi, mval in enumerate(M_list):
                    im_interferer = idx_map(b, 'M', mi)
                    for ti, tval in enumerate(T_list):
                        it_victim = idx_map(bp, 'T', ti)
                        # baseline interferer contribution
                        interf_base = gain_cache[(base_mi, base_ti)][b, ue_mask_victim]
                        interf_new = gain_cache[(mi, base_ti)][b, ue_mask_victim]
                        sig_victim = gain_cache[(base_mi, ti)][bp, ue_mask_victim]
                        sinr_base = sig_victim / (noise_W + interf_base)
                        sinr_new = sig_victim / (noise_W + interf_new)
                        delta_util = alpha * np.sum(np.log2(1 + sinr_new) - np.log2(1 + sinr_base))
                        # negative of utility change because we minimize x^T Q x
                        Q[im_interferer, it_victim] -= delta_util

    # One-hot penalties per parameter per cell
    for b in range(B):
        for ptype, plist in [('M', M_list), ('T', T_list), ('R', R_list)]:
            indices = [idx_map(b, ptype, i) for i in range(len(plist))]
            for i in indices:
                Q[i, i] -= lam_onehot
            for i in range(len(indices)):
                for j in range(i + 1, len(indices)):
                    Q[indices[i], indices[j]] += 2 * lam_onehot

    return Q

def simulated_annealing_qubo(Q, MTR_lists, B, steps=30000, T0=1.0, T1=1e-4):
    """
    Simulated annealing on QUBO with one-hot neighborhood moves.
    """
    M_list, T_list, R_list = MTR_lists
    n_m, n_t, n_r = len(M_list), len(T_list), len(R_list)
    n_per_b = n_m + n_t + n_r

    def idxs(b, mi, ti, ri):
        return b*n_per_b + mi, b*n_per_b + n_m + ti, b*n_per_b + n_m + n_t + ri

    # Random valid initial solution
    x = np.zeros(Q.shape[0], dtype=int)
    m_sels = np.random.randint(0, n_m, B)
    t_sels = np.random.randint(0, n_t, B)
    r_sels = np.random.randint(0, n_r, B)
    for b in range(B):
        im, it, ir = idxs(b, m_sels[b], t_sels[b], r_sels[b])
        x[im] = x[it] = x[ir] = 1

    def energy(xvec):
        return float(xvec.T @ Q @ xvec)

    E = energy(x)
    best_x = x.copy()
    best_E = E

    for step in range(steps):
        T = T0 * (T1/T0)**(step/max(1, steps-1))
        b = np.random.randint(0, B)
        ptype = np.random.choice(['M', 'T', 'R'])
        x_new = x.copy()

        if ptype == 'M':
            old = m_sels[b]
            cand = (old + np.random.randint(1, n_m)) % n_m
            im_old, _, _ = idxs(b, old, t_sels[b], r_sels[b])
            im_new, _, _ = idxs(b, cand, t_sels[b], r_sels[b])
            x_new[im_old] = 0
            x_new[im_new] = 1
        elif ptype == 'T':
            old = t_sels[b]
            cand = (old + np.random.randint(1, n_t)) % n_t
            _, it_old, _ = idxs(b, m_sels[b], old, r_sels[b])
            _, it_new, _ = idxs(b, m_sels[b], cand, r_sels[b])
            x_new[it_old] = 0
            x_new[it_new] = 1
        else:
            old = r_sels[b]
            cand = (old + np.random.randint(1, n_r)) % n_r
            _, _, ir_old = idxs(b, m_sels[b], t_sels[b], old)
            _, _, ir_new = idxs(b, m_sels[b], t_sels[b], cand)
            x_new[ir_old] = 0
            x_new[ir_new] = 1

        E_new = energy(x_new)
        dE = E_new - E
        if dE < 0 or np.random.rand() < math.exp(-dE / max(T, 1e-12)):
            x = x_new
            E = E_new
            if ptype == 'M':
                m_sels[b] = cand
            elif ptype == 'T':
                t_sels[b] = cand
            else:
                r_sels[b] = cand
            if E < best_E:
                best_E = E
                best_x = x.copy()

    return best_x

def decode_solution(x, MTR_lists, B):
    M_list, T_list, R_list = MTR_lists
    n_m, n_t, n_r = len(M_list), len(T_list), len(R_list)
    n_per_b = n_m + n_t + n_r
    M_sel = np.zeros(B, dtype=int)
    T_sel = np.zeros(B, dtype=int)
    R_sel = np.zeros(B, dtype=int)
    for b in range(B):
        seg = x[b*n_per_b:(b+1)*n_per_b]
        mvec = seg[:n_m]
        tvec = seg[n_m:n_m+n_t]
        rvec = seg[n_m+n_t:]
        M_sel[b] = M_list[np.argmax(mvec)] if mvec.sum() > 0 else M_list[0]
        T_sel[b] = T_list[np.argmax(tvec)] if tvec.sum() > 0 else T_list[0]
        R_sel[b] = R_list[np.argmax(rvec)] if rvec.sum() > 0 else R_list[0]
    return M_sel, T_sel, R_sel

# ------------------ Evaluation and Baselines ------------------

def evaluate_solution(M_sel, T_sel, R_sel, bs_xy, ue_xy):
    """
    Returns (sum_rate_gbps, p5_mbps, power_W, energy_eff_Mbps_per_W).
    """
    B = len(M_sel)
    G = get_link_gains(bs_xy, ue_xy, M_sel, T_sel)
    noise_W = db2lin(-174 + 10*np.log10(20e6))
    serving_bs = np.argmax(G, axis=0)

    user_sinr = np.zeros(ue_xy.shape[0], dtype=float)
    for k in range(ue_xy.shape[0]):
        b = serving_bs[k]
        sig = G[b, k]
        interf = np.sum(G[:, k]) - sig
        # simple pilot interference proxy if reuse > 1 for serving cell
        pilot_interf = 0.0
        if R_sel[b] > 1:
            for bp in range(B):
                if bp != b and (bp % R_sel[b]) == (b % R_sel[b]):
                    pilot_interf += G[bp, k]
        user_sinr[k] = sig / (noise_W + interf + pilot_interf)

    user_rates_Mbps = 20e6 * np.log2(1 + user_sinr) / 1e6
    sum_rate_gbps = float(np.sum(user_rates_Mbps) / 1000.0)
    p5_mbps = float(np.percentile(user_rates_Mbps, 5))
    power_W = float(np.sum(500.0 + 3.0*np.array(M_sel)))  # 500W static + 3W per RF chain
    energy_eff = float(np.sum(user_rates_Mbps) / power_W)
    return sum_rate_gbps, p5_mbps, power_W, energy_eff

def run_greedy_solver(bs_xy, ue_xy, M_choices, T_choices, R_choices):
    B = bs_xy.shape[0]
    M = np.random.choice(M_choices, B)
    T = np.random.choice(T_choices, B)
    R = np.random.choice(R_choices, B)

    for _ in range(3):  # 3 passes
        for b in range(B):
            best = (None, -1e9)
            for m in M_choices:
                for t in T_choices:
                    for r in R_choices:
                        M[b], T[b], R[b] = m, t, r
                        sr, p5, pw, ee = evaluate_solution(M, T, R, bs_xy, ue_xy)
                        util = sr - 0.005*pw  # proxy utility
                        if util > best[1]:
                            best = ((m, t, r), util)
            M[b], T[b], R[b] = best[0]
    return M, T, R

# ------------------ Main Experiment ------------------

def main():
    ap = argparse.ArgumentParser()
    ap.add_argument('--B', type=int, default=7)
    ap.add_argument('--runs', type=int, default=20)
    ap.add_argument('--traffic', type=str, default='hotspot', choices=['uniform','hotspot'])
    ap.add_argument('--steps', type=int, default=30000)
    ap.add_argument('--plot', action='store_true', help='Generate results_plot.pdf from coordinated QUBO-SA solution (first run)')
    args = ap.parse_args()

    M_choices = (32, 64, 128)
    T_choices = (6, 10, 14)
    R_choices = (1, 3)
    MTR_lists = (M_choices, T_choices, R_choices)

    methods = ['Static-Max', 'Greedy', 'QUBO-Indep', 'QUBO-Coordinated']
    results = {m: [] for m in methods}

    print(f'=== Simulation: B={args.B}, runs={args.runs}, traffic={args.traffic} ===')
    for run in range(args.runs):
        print(f'Run {run+1}/{args.runs}')
        seed_all(run)
        bs_xy = hex_grid(Bside=1, spacing_km=0.5)[:args.B]
        ue_xy = user_points(N=400, extent=1.0, traffic_type=args.traffic)

        # Static-Max
        M_sm = np.full(args.B, 128)
        T_sm = np.full(args.B, 10)
        R_sm = np.full(args.B, 1)
        results['Static-Max'].append(evaluate_solution(M_sm, T_sm, R_sm, bs_xy, ue_xy))

        # Greedy
        M_g, T_g, R_g = run_greedy_solver(bs_xy, ue_xy, M_choices, T_choices, R_choices)
        results['Greedy'].append(evaluate_solution(M_g, T_g, R_g, bs_xy, ue_xy))

        # QUBO Independent
        Q_indep = build_qubo(args.B, M_choices, T_choices, R_choices, bs_xy, ue_xy, cell_independent=True)
        x_indep = simulated_annealing_qubo(Q_indep, MTR_lists, args.B, steps=args.steps)
        M_i, T_i, R_i = decode_solution(x_indep, MTR_lists, args.B)
        results['QUBO-Indep'].append(evaluate_solution(M_i, T_i, R_i, bs_xy, ue_xy))

        # QUBO Coordinated
        Q_coord = build_qubo(args.B, M_choices, T_choices, R_choices, bs_xy, ue_xy, cell_independent=False)
        x_coord = simulated_annealing_qubo(Q_coord, MTR_lists, args.B, steps=args.steps)
        M_c, T_c, R_c = decode_solution(x_coord, MTR_lists, args.B)
        results['QUBO-Coordinated'].append(evaluate_solution(M_c, T_c, R_c, bs_xy, ue_xy))

        if args.plot and run == 0:
            plot_config(bs_xy, ue_xy, M_c, T_c, args.traffic)

    # Print summary
    kpis = ['Sum Rate (Gbps)', '5th Pctl Rate (Mbps)', 'Power (W)', 'Energy Eff (Mbps/W)']
    print('\n--- Results Summary (mean +/- std) ---')
    for m in methods:
        arr = np.array(results[m])
        mean = arr.mean(axis=0)
        std = arr.std(axis=0)
        print(f'{m:>16}:  SR={mean[0]:.2f}±{std[0]:.2f}  P5={mean[1]:.2f}±{std[1]:.2f}  P={mean[2]:.1f}±{std[2]:.1f}  EE={mean[3]:.2f}±{std[3]:.2f}')

if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
