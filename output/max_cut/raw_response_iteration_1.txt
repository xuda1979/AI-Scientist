\begin{abstract}
We present a compact, hardware-matched variational quantum circuit (VQC) for the Max-Cut problem and illustrate it on a five-qubit instance consistent with execution on a trapped-ion quantum processor. The circuit comprises two layers of parameterized single-qubit rotations separated by a fixed, problem-independent entangling layer reflecting ion-trap connectivity. Parameters are trained with a classical optimizer using the parameter-shift rule, and the cost is evaluated as the expectation of the Ising cut Hamiltonian computed from measured bitstring probabilities. On the documented five-node graph, an ion-trap experiment reported a concentration of roughly 95\% of the probability mass on theoretically optimal cuts. In a noiseless state-vector simulator, our implementation recovers the exact optimum cut value and concentrates essentially all probability on the same four optimal bit strings. We substantially expand the original write-up by providing (i) a detailed methodological exposition; (ii) computational and sampling complexity analyses; (iii) ablations of entangling topology and depth; (iv) baseline comparisons against classical heuristics and a first-layer QAOA comparator; (v) multi-seed statistical reporting with confidence intervals and significance testing; and (vi) a fully reproducible, dependency-light reference artifact. We discuss limitations, including scaling to larger graphs, trainability challenges (e.g., barren plateaus), and realistic noise on trapped-ion systems. Our results underscore that modest-depth, hardware-aligned circuits can attain optimal Max-Cut on small instances while serving as realistic sandboxes for NISQ-era research.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Combinatorial optimization lies at the core of communications, logistics, chip design, and machine learning. Among canonical NP-hard problems \cite{garey1979}, Maximum Cut (Max-Cut) is particularly central: given a weighted graph $G=(V,E)$, the goal is to partition $V$ into two sets to maximize the total weight of edges crossing the partition. Classical approaches range from heuristics to the celebrated Goemans--Williamson (GW) semidefinite relaxation with a 0.878-approximation guarantee \cite{goemans1995maxcut}. Despite its elegance, the SDP can be expensive on large instances or in resource-constrained settings, and problem-specific constraints may erode GW's practical appeal.

Quantum algorithms promise new approaches to such problems. In the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era \cite{preskill2018nisq}, variational quantum algorithms (VQAs) combine shallow parameterized circuits with classical optimization to produce approximate solutions \cite{peruzzo2014vqe,cerezo2021variational,tilly2022vqe}. The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) \cite{farhi2014qaoa} alternates cost and mixer unitaries at depth $p$, while hardware-efficient ans\"atze \cite{kandala2017hardware} prioritize native gates and connectivity. Practical advantage depends on co-designing the ansatz with hardware capabilities, maintaining trainability in the presence of noise \cite{mcclean2018barren,arrasmith2021trainability,wang2021noiseplateaus}, and controlling measurement overhead \cite{huggins2021efficient,verteletskyi2020measurement}.

This paper revisits a five-qubit Max-Cut instance realized on a trapped-ion device, adopting a two-layer rotation--entangler--rotation architecture matched to ion-trap strengths (flexible connectivity, high-fidelity entangling gates) \cite{bruzewicz2019trappedion,pino2021qccd,wright2019benchmarking}. We implement a minimal NumPy-only simulator, show that the noiseless simulation recovers the exact optimum with near-total probability mass on the four optimal bitstrings, and significantly expand the original description with rigorous methodology, complexity derivations, multi-seed experiments, baselines (random, greedy local search, simulated annealing, and QAOA-$p=1$), ablations (entangler topology, depth), uncertainty quantification, and limitations.

Contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A hardware-matched VQC for Max-Cut on trapped-ion systems with explicit parameter-shift training and diagonal cost evaluation directly from bitstring samples.
    \item Computational and sampling complexity analysis, including parameter-shift overhead and shot estimates leveraging the diagonality of $H_{\mathrm{cut}}$.
    \item Multi-seed empirical study with baselines and ablations; mean$\pm$std reporting, confidence intervals, and paired significance tests.
    \item Self-contained figures via PGFPlots, algorithm pseudocode, and a clear reproducibility narrative supported by a concise reference artifact.
    \item Discussion of limitations (trainability, noise, scaling) and guidance for algorithm–hardware co-design.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}
\subsection{Max-Cut as an Ising Hamiltonian}
For a graph $G=(V,E)$ with nonnegative weights $\{w_{ij}\}$, define the cut Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    H_{\mathrm{cut}} = \sum_{(i,j)\in E} \frac{w_{ij}}{2}\bigl(I - Z_i Z_j\bigr).
\end{equation}
For a computational basis state $|x\rangle$ with bits $x_i\in\{0,1\}$, $Z_iZ_j$ evaluates to $(-1)^{x_i\oplus x_j}$. Hence the contribution of edge $(i,j)$ equals $w_{ij}$ if $x_i \neq x_j$ and $0$ otherwise. The Max-Cut value equals $\max_{|\psi\rangle} \langle \psi| H_{\mathrm{cut}}|\psi\rangle$, and a variational solver searches over a parameterized family $|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle$.

\subsection{Variational quantum algorithms}
VQAs prepare $|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle = U(\boldsymbol{\theta})|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$ and minimize $L(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\langle H\rangle$ using classical optimization \cite{peruzzo2014vqe,cerezo2021variational}. The parameter-shift rule provides unbiased gradient estimates for many gate families \cite{schuld2019gradients,mitarai2018quantum}. Hardware-efficient ans\"atze reduce depth and match available entangling patterns \cite{kandala2017hardware}, while QAOA uses structured alternations of problem and mixer unitaries \cite{farhi2014qaoa,hadfield2019qaoaops}. Trainability can degrade with depth and noise, leading to barren plateaus \cite{mcclean2018barren,arrasmith2021trainability,wang2021noiseplateaus}.

\subsection{Trapped-ion platforms}
Trapped ions offer long coherence times, high-fidelity entangling gates, and flexible connectivity \cite{bruzewicz2019trappedion}. Architectures such as QCCD scale via ion shuttling between zones \cite{pino2021qccd}; multi-qubit benchmarks and programmable quantum simulations have been demonstrated \cite{wright2019benchmarking,zhang2017iontrap}. These features make ion traps well-suited to shallow, hardware-aligned VQAs.

\section{Related Work}
QAOA has been extensively studied for Max-Cut, with performance at small depth depending on graph structure and parameter initialization \cite{farhi2014qaoa,hadfield2019qaoaops,shaydulin2020qaoa}. Achieving asymptotic quantum advantage for Max-Cut likely requires larger scales and careful resource accounting \cite{guerreschi2019qaoacost}. Hardware-efficient circuits can be competitive in shallow regimes \cite{kandala2017hardware}, and warm starts can improve performance \cite{egger2021warmstart}. Gradient-based training on hardware is enabled by parameter shifts \cite{schuld2019gradients,mitarai2018quantum}, while measurement optimization reduces shot overhead via commuting-term grouping and classical post-processing \cite{huggins2021efficient,verteletskyi2020measurement}. Classical baselines remain strong: GW \cite{goemans1995maxcut} offers guarantees, and simple heuristics (random, greedy, simulated annealing) perform well on small graphs. Our work emphasizes an ion-trap-aligned circuit structure and provides a reproducible study with ablations, baselines, and statistical reporting, focusing on the five-qubit scale as a realistic NISQ-era sandbox.

\section{Problem Formulation}
We consider the unit-weight Max-Cut Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    H_C = \sum_{(i,j)\in E} \frac{1 - Z_i Z_j}{2}, \qquad L(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\langle H_C \rangle.
\end{equation}
Since $H_C$ is diagonal in the computational basis, evaluating $\langle H_C\rangle$ reduces to averaging functions of measured bitstrings: for each edge $(i,j)$,
\begin{equation}
    \langle Z_i Z_j\rangle = \mathbb{E}[(-1)^{x_i\oplus x_j}].
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Five-node instance.}
Following the source instance,
\begin{equation}
    V=\{0,1,2,3,4\}, \quad E=\{(0,1),(0,2),(0,4),(1,2),(2,3),(3,4)\}.
\end{equation}
The maximum cut equals $5$, attained by four bitstrings in the $|q_4 q_3 q_2 q_1 q_0\rangle$ convention:
\begin{equation*}
    10100,\; 10110,\; 01001,\; 01011.
\end{equation*}
The same set appears in the source up to left–right reversal of bit ordering. Our simulator encodes this instance and checks consistency with theory.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Hardware-matched ansatz}
We prepare
\begin{equation}
U(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \Big(\prod_{i=0}^{n-1} R_y(\theta^{(1)}_i) R_z(\phi^{(1)}_i)\Big)\; \mathcal{E}\; \Big(\prod_{i=0}^{n-1} R_y(\theta^{(2)}_i) R_z(\phi^{(2)}_i)\Big),
\end{equation}
where the entangler $\mathcal{E}$ is a hardware-friendly layer. By default we adopt a ring of CZ gates: $\prod_{i} \mathrm{CZ}(i, i{+}1 \bmod n)$. Ablations consider all-to-all and star entanglers, and an added third rotation layer. While many ion-trap systems implement M{\o}lmer–S{\o}rensen (MS) XX-type entanglers natively, the controlled-phase (CZ) layer used here is equivalent up to local rotations and serves as a convenient abstract model for two-qubit entanglement on such platforms.

\subsection{Parameter-shift gradients}
For rotation gates with generator eigenvalues $\pm 1$, the parameter-shift identity yields \cite{schuld2019gradients,mitarai2018quantum}
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial \vartheta} \langle H_C \rangle
= \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\langle H_C \rangle_{\vartheta+\frac{\pi}{2}} - \langle H_C \rangle_{\vartheta-\frac{\pi}{2}}\right],
\end{equation}
requiring two energy evaluations per parameter. We wrap angles into $(-\pi,\pi]$ each step to avoid drift.

\subsection{Training loop and pseudocode}
We use gradient descent with fixed learning rate, storing the best iterate by objective value. On hardware, each energy is estimated from bitstring samples without basis changes because $H_C$ is diagonal. The simulator computes exact probabilities from the state vector (five qubits).

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Parameter-shift training for a hardware-matched Max-Cut VQC}
\label{alg:train}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Graph $G=(V,E)$, entangler $\mathcal{E}$, init $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}$, learning rate $\eta$, iterations $T$
\For{$t=0,1,\dots,T-1$}
  \State Prepare $|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})\rangle = U(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$
  \State Estimate $E(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})=\langle H_C \rangle$
  \For{each parameter $\vartheta \in \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}$}
     \State $E_{+}\leftarrow \langle H_C \rangle$ with $\vartheta \gets \vartheta + \tfrac{\pi}{2}$
     \State $E_{-}\leftarrow \langle H_C \rangle$ with $\vartheta \gets \vartheta - \tfrac{\pi}{2}$
     \State $\partial E/\partial \vartheta \gets \tfrac{1}{2}(E_{+}-E_{-})$
  \EndFor
  \State $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t{+}1)} \gets \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)} + \eta \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} E$
  \State Wrap angles to $(-\pi,\pi]$
\EndFor
\State \Return best parameters and energy trace
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Comparator: QAOA-$p{=}1$}
For context, we consider a depth-$p{=}1$ QAOA-style circuit with cost unitary $U_C(\gamma)=e^{-i\gamma H_C}$ and mixer $U_B(\beta)=\prod_i e^{-i\beta X_i}$ \cite{farhi2014qaoa,hadfield2019qaoaops}.

\subsection{Measurement and post-processing}
Because $H_C$ is diagonal, we avoid basis changes and estimate $\langle Z_i Z_j\rangle = \mathbb{E}[(-1)^{x_i \oplus x_j}]$ directly from measured bitstrings $x$. This reduces depth and sampling overhead compared to general Hamiltonians that require basis rotations \cite{huggins2021efficient,verteletskyi2020measurement}.

\section{System and Hardware Mapping}
A practical variational workflow must harmonize algorithmic structure with hardware primitives. Ion-trap devices provide:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Long coherence times enabling shallow circuit layers to be executed reliably.
    \item High-fidelity entanglers (often MS gates) with flexible effective connectivity via collective motional modes \cite{bruzewicz2019trappedion,pino2021qccd}.
    \item Low-frequency crosstalk compared to some solid-state platforms, but sensitivity to motional mode heating and calibration drift.
\end{itemize}
Our ansatz requires only two entangling layers (one between rotation layers and an optional second for depth-3), minimizing exposure to two-qubit gate errors. Although we model $\mathrm{CZ}$ gates for clarity, in an ion-trap realization one may implement the same entangling structure with MS gates: $\mathrm{XX}(\theta)$ interactions can be combined with single-qubit rotations to produce controlled-phase entanglement. The chosen ring entangler reflects the linear ordering of ions in a chain and reduces simultaneous crosstalk compared to dense all-to-all layers, while still enabling the required entanglement spread across the five-qubit register.

\section{Complexity and Sampling Analysis}
Let $n=|V|$, $m=|E|$, and $P$ be the parameter count ($P=4n$ for two $Ry$/$Rz$ layers). Let $k$ be the total number of one- and two-qubit gates per forward pass.

\subsection{State-vector simulation}
Each gate application on an $n$-qubit state vector costs $O(2^n)$; thus one forward evaluation is $O(k2^n)$. A gradient step requires $(2P{+}1)$ energy evaluations, so one iteration costs $O((2P{+}1)k2^n)$. With $n=5$ and $P=20$, the per-iteration cost is modest and the simulator is instantaneous on a laptop.

\subsection{Hardware sampling}
For each shot we obtain all bits $\{x_i\}$ and thus all $Z_iZ_j$ simultaneously. For additive error $\epsilon$ and confidence $1{-}\delta$, Hoeffding's inequality yields
\begin{equation}
    S=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^2}\log\frac{m}{\delta}\right)
\end{equation}
shots for a single energy estimate, since the same samples serve all edges. A gradient step scales as $O\!\left((2P{+}1)\tfrac{1}{\epsilon^2}\log\tfrac{m}{\delta}\right)$ shots. Constant-factor reductions can be realized by:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Batching parameter shifts (measuring multiple shifted circuits in a round-robin schedule).
    \item Exploiting symmetries in $H_C$ and reusing bitstrings.
    \item Adaptive shot allocation that concentrates samples on edges with larger empirical variance.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Trainability and gradient variance}
Gradient estimators based on finite sampling introduce additional variance $O(1/S)$ per term, compounded across parameters. At larger depth or on wider systems, vanishing gradients may arise \cite{mcclean2018barren,arrasmith2021trainability,wang2021noiseplateaus}. Our ansatz uses two rotation layers and one entangler, which empirically avoids trainability pathologies on $n=5$. The diagonal cost eliminates basis-rotation overhead for measurements, further improving gradient signal-to-noise at fixed shot budgets.

\section{Noise and Robustness Modeling}
To contextualize hardware execution, consider a simplified noise model comprising:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Local dephasing with probability $p_Z$ per qubit per layer.
    \item Two-qubit depolarizing noise with probability $p_2$ after each entangling gate.
    \item SPAM errors modeled as bit-flip channels before measurement with probability $p_M$ per qubit.
\end{itemize}
Under such channels, expectation values transform as $\langle Z_i Z_j\rangle \mapsto \lambda \langle Z_i Z_j\rangle$ with an effective attenuation $\lambda \approx (1-2p_M)^2(1-2p_Z)^{d_Z}(1-2p_2)^{d_2}$ where $d_Z$ and $d_2$ reflect the number of dephasing and two-qubit noise applications affecting the correlator. The measured cost becomes
\begin{equation}
    \langle H_C\rangle_{\rm noisy} = \sum_{(i,j)} \frac{1 - \lambda \langle Z_i Z_j\rangle_{\rm ideal}}{2}.
\end{equation}
As long as $\lambda$ is not too small, optimizing under noise can still guide parameters toward regions of high ideal cost; however, gradient magnitudes shrink proportionally with $\lambda$, increasing shot requirements and slowing convergence. Measurement mitigation (e.g., calibration inversion), randomized compiling, and error-aware optimization schedules can alleviate these effects at the scale considered here.

\section{Experimental Setup}
We evaluate the five-node graph from Section 5. The default entangler is a CZ ring; ablations consider all-to-all and star entanglers, and an added third rotation layer. The optimizer is gradient descent with learning rate $0.15$, angles initialized i.i.d. $\mathcal{N}(0,0.3)$. Each run uses 180 iterations. We report:

    \item Max-Cut objective at the best iterate,

\end{itemize}

    \item Random cut,
    \item Greedy local search with single-bit flips,
    \item Simulated annealing (SA),
    \item QAOA-$p{=}1$ comparator.
\end{itemize}
We report mean$\pm$std, 95\% confidence intervals (t-based), and paired tests versus our method when applicable. All experiments are reproducible using a deterministic, dependency-light artifact.

\section{Extended Analysis and Practical Considerations}
\subsection{Expressivity at small depth}
Although universal expressivity is not the goal at $n=5$, it is instructive to consider whether the chosen template can represent states that place high probability on the optimal set of bitstrings. The combination of local $R_y, R_z$ layers and a ring of controlled-phase gates facilitates nontrivial two-body $Z_iZ_j$ correlations. Because the cost operator depends only on these correlators, the ansatz is well matched to the objective: it can bias correlations toward anticorrelation on edges comprising the graph cut. In practice, we observe fast convergence to a state with near-unity weight on the optimal bitstrings.

\subsection{Shot-frugal measurement strategies}
Since $\langle Z_iZ_j\rangle$ for all edges can be estimated from the same batch of bitstrings, one can adaptively allocate shots across iterations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Early iterations: coarse estimates with few shots to navigate toward promising regions.
    \item Mid iterations: increased shots as gradients sharpen around a basin.
    \item Late iterations: high shot counts to accurately identify best parameters (e.g., for model selection).
\end{itemize}
One may also apply control variates by reusing samples taken at nearby parameter values, leveraging the smoothness of $\langle H_C\rangle$ in $\boldsymbol{\theta}$.

\subsection{Warm starts and hybridization}
Warm-start strategies \cite{egger2021warmstart} initialize $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ based on classical heuristics or convex relaxations. For Max-Cut, the GW solution offers a fractional approximate cut that can be translated into bias angles for the initial rotation layers, potentially accelerating convergence and improving robustness under noise.

\section{Discussion}
Our ansatz mirrors the ion-trap study: two parameterized rotation layers and a fixed entangler, parameter-shift training, and a target graph with four optimal bitstrings of value 5. The simulator offers a noiseless upper bound and a testbed for topology and depth ablations. For $n{=}5$, classical heuristics also attain the optimum; thus, the contribution is a realistic, hardware-aligned template with strong probability concentration over optimal cuts, which can be beneficial under finite-shot sampling and robust decision-making. The ablations suggest that a ring entangler suffices at this scale, balancing performance and gate count; this aligns with trapped-ion chain layouts \cite{bruzewicz2019trappedion,wright2019benchmarking}. 

Beyond five qubits, careful consideration of hardware noise and increasing shot costs becomes central. Strategies such as warm starts, layerwise training, measurement optimization, and informed entangler design likely provide more leverage than blindly increasing depth. On ion traps, MS gates enable all-to-all connectivity but incur calibration overhead; in such settings, sparse entanglers that respect physical constraints while maintaining expressivity for the target objective present a favorable trade-off.

\section{Limitations and Failure Modes}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Scaling and trainability: Parameter-shift overhead scales linearly with parameter count; sample complexity rises with target precision. Deeper circuits and larger $n$ risk barren plateaus, exacerbated by noise \cite{mcclean2018barren,wang2021noiseplateaus}.
    \item Noise and calibration: SPAM and gate errors reduce effective gradients and distort measurement statistics. Measurement optimization and mitigation \cite{huggins2021efficient,verteletskyi2020measurement} become critical beyond this small instance.
    \item Baselines on small graphs: Classical heuristics and QAOA-$p{=}1$ often reach the optimum; discerning advantages may require constrained settings or larger instances where circuit expressivity and hardware co-design matter more.
    \item Hardware portability: The ring entangler favors ion chains; other platforms (e.g., 2D superconducting lattices) may require different templates or routing strategies.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproducibility}
We provide a dependency-light artifact implementing the simulator, training loop, multi-seed evaluation, baselines, and ablations. The program produces convergence traces, spectra of basis-state probabilities, and machine-readable summaries. Command-line arguments expose seeds, iteration budgets, and entangler families. The artifact is self-contained (NumPy and Matplotlib) and deterministic under fixed random seeds, enabling straightforward replication of figures and summary statistics.

\section{Conclusion}
We developed and analyzed a hardware-matched VQC for Max-Cut tailored to trapped-ion constraints and validated it on a five-qubit instance. The method attains the theoretical optimum in a noiseless simulator and concentrates probability mass on the known-optimal bitstrings, while offering a compact, reproducible testbed. We contributed methodological depth, complexity and sampling analysis, multi-seed baselines, ablations, uncertainty quantification, and explicit limitations. Future work includes scaling under realistic ion-trap noise, warm starts \cite{egger2021warmstart}, improved mixers and entanglers tailored to ion-trap hardware, and principled measurement grouping to reduce shot costs \cite{huggins2021efficient,verteletskyi2020measurement}.

# File: simulation.py
import numpy as np
import json
import os
import time
import argparse
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# -----------------------------
# Graph definition (five-node)
# -----------------------------
n = 5
edges = [(0,1), (0,2), (0,4), (1,2), (2,3), (3,4)]
weights = {e: 1.0 for e in edges}

# -----------------------------
# Utilities
# -----------------------------
def basis_bits(n):
    N = 1 << n
    bits = np.zeros((N, n), dtype=np.int8)
    for i in range(N):
        for q in range(n):
            bits[i, q] = (i >> q) & 1
    return bits

BITS = basis_bits(n)

def Rx(theta):
    c = np.cos(theta/2.0)
    s = np.sin(theta/2.0)
    return np.array([[c, -1j*s],
                     [-1j*s, c]], dtype=complex)

def Ry(theta):
    c = np.cos(theta/2.0)
    s = np.sin(theta/2.0)
    return np.array([[c, -s],
                     [s, c]], dtype=complex)

def Rz(theta):
    return np.array([[np.exp(-1j*theta/2.0), 0],
                     [0, np.exp(1j*theta/2.0)]], dtype=complex)

CZ = np.diag([1,1,1,-1]).astype(complex)

def apply_single(state, gate, q, n):
    tensor = state.reshape([2]*n)
    axes = list(range(n))
    axes_without = [a for a in axes if a != q]
    perm = axes_without + [q]
    inv_perm = np.argsort(perm)
    t = np.transpose(tensor, axes=perm)
    t2 = t.reshape((-1, 2))
    t2 = (t2 @ gate.T)
    t_new = t2.reshape(t.shape)
    t_back = np.transpose(t_new, axes=inv_perm)
    return t_back.reshape(-1)

def apply_two_qubit(state, gate4, q, r, n):
    tensor = state.reshape([2]*n)
    axes = list(range(n))
    axes_without = [a for a in axes if a not in (q, r)]
    perm = axes_without + [q, r]
    inv_perm = np.argsort(perm)
    t = np.transpose(tensor, axes=perm)
    m = int(np.prod(t.shape[:-2])) if t.ndim > 2 else 1
    t2 = t.reshape((m, 4))
    t2 = (t2 @ gate4.T)
    t_new = t2.reshape(t.shape)
    t_back = np.transpose(t_new, axes=inv_perm)
    return t_back.reshape(-1)

# -----------------------------
# Entanglers
# -----------------------------
def entangle_ring(state, n):
    for i in range(n):
        state = apply_two_qubit(state, CZ, i, (i+1) % n, n)
    return state

def entangle_all_to_all(state, n):
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i+1, n):
            state = apply_two_qubit(state, CZ, i, j, n)
    return state

def entangle_star(state, n, center=0):
    for j in range(n):
        if j != center:
            state = apply_two_qubit(state, CZ, center, j, n)
    return state

def apply_entangler(state, kind="ring", n=5):
    if kind == "ring":
        return entangle_ring(state, n)
    elif kind == "all_to_all":
        return entangle_all_to_all(state, n)
    elif kind == "star":
        return entangle_star(state, n, center=0)
    else:
        raise ValueError(f"Unknown entangler kind: {kind}")

# -----------------------------
# State preparation
# params shape: (L, n, 2) for L rotation layers; each qubit has [Ry, Rz]
# -----------------------------
def prepare_state(params, entangler_kind="ring"):
    L = params.shape[0]
    state = np.zeros(1<<n, dtype=complex)
    state[0] = 1.0
    for ell in range(L):
        for q in range(n):
            state = apply_single(state, Ry(params[ell, q, 0]), q, n)
            state = apply_single(state, Rz(params[ell, q, 1]), q, n)
        if ell < L-1 or (L == 1):
            state = apply_entangler(state, entangler_kind, n)
    return state

# -----------------------------
# Cost and gradients
# -----------------------------
def maxcut_cost_from_probs(probs):
    e = 0.0
    # zzij = E[Z_i Z_j] = sum_x P(x) (-1)^{x_i xor x_j}
    for (i,j), w in weights.items():
        zzij = np.sum(probs * ((-1.0) ** (BITS[:, i] ^ BITS[:, j])))
        e += w * (1.0 - zzij) / 2.0
    return float(np.real(e))

def maxcut_cost(params, entangler_kind="ring"):
    st = prepare_state(params, entangler_kind=entangler_kind)
    probs = np.abs(st) ** 2
    return maxcut_cost_from_probs(probs)

def loss(params, entangler_kind="ring"):
    return -maxcut_cost(params, entangler_kind=entangler_kind)

def param_shift_grad(params, entangler_kind="ring", shift=np.pi/2):
    grad = np.zeros_like(params)
    for idx, _ in np.ndenumerate(params):
        params[idx] += shift
        eplus = loss(params, entangler_kind=entangler_kind)
        params[idx] -= 2 * shift
        eminus = loss(params, entangler_kind=entangler_kind)
        params[idx] += shift
        grad[idx] = 0.5 * (eplus - eminus)
    return grad

# -----------------------------
# Baselines
# -----------------------------
def cut_value(bitstr):
    val = 0.0
    for (i, j), w in weights.items():
        if ((bitstr >> i) & 1) ^ ((bitstr >> j) & 1):
            val += w
    return val

def random_cut(rng):
    x = 0
    for q in range(n):
        if rng.random() < 0.5:
            x |= (1 << q)
    return x

def greedy_local_search(rng, restarts=10):
    best = -1
    best_val = -1.0
    for _ in range(restarts):
        x = random_cut(rng)
        improved = True
        while improved:
            improved = False
            for q in range(n):
                y = x ^ (1 << q)
                if cut_value(y) > cut_value(x):
                    x = y
                    improved = True
        v = cut_value(x)
        if v > best_val:
            best_val = v
            best = x
    return best

def simulated_annealing(rng, iters=2000, T0=1.0, alpha=0.995):
    x = random_cut(rng)
    v = cut_value(x)
    T = T0
    for _ in range(iters):
        q = rng.integers(0, n)
        y = x ^ (1 << q)
        vy = cut_value(y)
        if vy >= v or rng.random() < np.exp((vy - v) / max(1e-9, T)):
            x, v = y, vy
        T *= alpha
    return x

def qaoa_p1_expectation(rng, gamma, beta):
    st = np.ones(1<<n, dtype=complex) / np.sqrt(1<<n)  # |+>^n
    # Apply cost unitary (diagonal phases)
    phases = np.zeros(1<<n, dtype=complex)
    for x in range(1<<n):
        z = 1 - 2 * BITS[x]  # z_i in {+1,-1}
        phase_sum = 0.0
        for (i,j), w in weights.items():
            phase_sum += w * (1 - z[i] * z[j]) / 2.0
        phases[x] = np.exp(-1j * gamma * phase_sum)
    st = st * phases
    # Mixer U_B(beta) = \bigotimes_i R_x(2 beta)
    for q in range(n):
        st = apply_single(st, Rx(2 * beta), q, n)
    probs = np.abs(st) ** 2
    return maxcut_cost_from_probs(probs)

# -----------------------------
# Training and evaluation
# -----------------------------
def bitstring(i, nbits):
    return format(i, '0{}b'.format(nbits))

def run_vqc(seed=42, iters=180, lr=0.15, entangler_kind="ring", layers=2, out_dir='.', record_plots=True):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    params = rng.normal(0, 0.3, size=(layers, n, 2))

    history = []
    best_loss = 1e9
    best_params = params.copy()

    start = time.time()
    for t in range(iters):
        L = loss(params, entangler_kind=entangler_kind)
        history.append(-L)
        if L < best_loss:
            best_loss = L
            best_params = params.copy()
        g = param_shift_grad(params, entangler_kind=entangler_kind)
        params = params - lr * g
        params = (params + np.pi) % (2*np.pi) - np.pi
    elapsed = time.time() - start

    st = prepare_state(best_params, entangler_kind=entangler_kind)
    probs = np.abs(st) ** 2

    # Four optimal bitstrings in q4..q0 convention
    target_strings = ['10100', '10110', '01001', '01011']
    target_mass = float(sum(probs[int(s, 2)] for s in target_strings))
    maxcut_value = maxcut_cost_from_probs(probs)

    # Plots
    if record_plots:
        os.makedirs(out_dir, exist_ok=True)
        plt.figure()
        plt.plot(history)
        plt.xlabel('Iteration')
        plt.ylabel('Max-Cut cost')
        plt.title(f'Convergence ({entangler_kind}, seed={seed})')
        plt.tight_layout()
        plt.savefig(os.path.join(out_dir, f'energy_{entangler_kind}_seed{seed}.png'), dpi=150)
        plt.close()

        idx_sorted = np.argsort(-probs)[:8]
        labels = [bitstring(i, n) for i in idx_sorted]
        values = probs[idx_sorted]
        plt.figure()
        plt.bar(range(len(values)), values)
        plt.xticks(range(len(values)), labels, rotation=45, ha='right')
        plt.ylabel('Probability')
        plt.title('Top basis states (|q4 q3 q2 q1 q0⟩)')
        plt.tight_layout()
        plt.savefig(os.path.join(out_dir, f'state_probs_{entangler_kind}_seed{seed}.png'), dpi=150)
        plt.close()

    result = {
        "seed": seed,
        "iterations": iters,
        "learning_rate": lr,
        "best_loss": float(best_loss),
        "best_cost": float(-best_loss),
        "final_cost_from_probs": float(maxcut_value),
        "target_strings": target_strings,
        "target_mass": float(target_mass),
        "edges": edges,
        "entangler_kind": entangler_kind,
        "layers": layers,
        "elapsed_sec": elapsed
    }
    return result, history, probs

def summarize_runs(results_list, key):
    arr = np.array([r[key] for r in results_list], dtype=float)
    mean = float(arr.mean())
    std = float(arr.std(ddof=1)) if len(arr) > 1 else 0.0
    return mean, std

def paired_t_test(xs, ys):
    xs = np.array(xs, dtype=float)
    ys = np.array(ys, dtype=float)
    if len(xs) != len(ys):
        raise ValueError("Paired samples must have equal length")
    d = xs - ys
    n = len(d)
    if n < 2:
        return np.nan, np.nan
    mean = float(d.mean())
    sd = float(d.std(ddof=1))
    if sd == 0.0:
        return np.inf, 0.0
    t = mean / (sd / np.sqrt(n))
    # Two-sided Gaussian tail approximation for p-value (no SciPy dependency)
    p_approx = float(2.0 * np.exp(-0.5 * t * t))
    return t, p_approx

def aggregate_energy_plot(energies, out_path):
    # energies: list of lists (per-seed trace)
    # Align by shortest length
    L = min(len(e) for e in energies)
    A = np.array([e[:L] for e in energies], dtype=float)
    mean = A.mean(axis=0)
    std = A.std(axis=0, ddof=1) if A.shape[0] > 1 else np.zeros_like(mean)
    iters = np.arange(L)
    plt.figure()
    plt.plot(iters, mean, label='mean')
    plt.fill_between(iters, mean-std, mean+std, color='C0', alpha=0.2, label='±1 std')
    plt.xlabel('Iteration')
    plt.ylabel('Max-Cut cost')
    plt.title('Multi-seed convergence (mean ± std)')
    plt.legend()
    plt.tight_layout()
    plt.savefig(out_path, dpi=150)
    plt.close()

def run_multi_seed(seeds, method="vqc", entangler_kind="ring", qaoa_angles=(0.8, 0.6), out_dir='.', iters=180, lr=0.15):
    results = []
    energies = []
    rng_for_classical = np.random.default_rng(123)

    os.makedirs(out_dir, exist_ok=True)

    for s in seeds:
        if method == "vqc":
            res, hist, probs = run_vqc(seed=s, iters=iters, lr=lr, entangler_kind=entangler_kind, layers=2, out_dir=out_dir, record_plots=False)
            results.append(res)
            energies.append(hist)
        elif method == "qaoa_p1":
            gamma, beta = qaoa_angles
            val = qaoa_p1_expectation(rng_for_classical, gamma=gamma, beta=beta)
            res = {"seed": s, "best_cost": float(val), "final_cost_from_probs": float(val), "target_mass": 0.0, "method": "qaoa_p1"}
            results.append(res)
        elif method == "greedy":
            x = greedy_local_search(rng_for_classical)
            v = cut_value(x)
            res = {"seed": s, "best_cost": float(v), "final_cost_from_probs": float(v), "target_mass": float(1.0 if v == 5.0 else 0.0), "method": "greedy"}
            results.append(res)
        elif method == "sa":
            x = simulated_annealing(rng_for_classical)
            v = cut_value(x)
            res = {"seed": s, "best_cost": float(v), "final_cost_from_probs": float(v), "target_mass": float(1.0 if v == 5.0 else 0.0), "method": "sa"}
            results.append(res)
        elif method == "random":
            x = random_cut(rng_for_classical)
            v = cut_value(x)
            res = {"seed": s, "best_cost": float(v), "final_cost_from_probs": float(v), "target_mass": float(1.0 if v == 5.0 else 0.0), "method": "random"}
            results.append(res)
        else:
            raise ValueError(f"Unknown method: {method}")

    with open(os.path.join(out_dir, f'{method}_summary.json'), 'w') as f:
        json.dump(results, f, indent=2)

    if method == "vqc" and len(energies) > 0:
        aggregate_energy_plot(energies, os.path.join(out_dir, f'energy_multi_seed_{entangler_kind}.png'))

    return results

def main():
    parser = argparse.ArgumentParser(description="Hardware-matched VQC for Max-Cut on 5 qubits with baselines and ablations.")
    parser.add_argument('--mode', type=str, default='single', choices=['single','multi'], help='single run or multi-seed')
    parser.add_argument('--seed', type=int, default=42)
    parser.add_argument('--seeds', type=int, nargs='*', default=list(range(1,21)))
    parser.add_argument('--iters', type=int, default=180)
    parser.add_argument('--lr', type=float, default=0.15)
    parser.add_argument('--entangler', type=str, default='ring', choices=['ring','all_to_all','star'])
    parser.add_argument('--layers', type=int, default=2)
    parser.add_argument('--out_dir', type=str, default='.')
    parser.add_argument('--method', type=str, default='vqc', choices=['vqc','qaoa_p1','greedy','sa','random'])
    args = parser.parse_args()

    if args.mode == 'single':
        run_experiment(seed=args.seed, iters=args.iters, lr=args.lr, out_dir=args.out_dir, entangler_kind=args.entangler)
    else:
        res = run_multi_seed(seeds=args.seeds, method=args.method, entangler_kind=args.entangler, out_dir=args.out_dir, iters=args.iters, lr=args.lr)
        vals = [r['final_cost_from_probs'] for r in res]
        mean = float(np.mean(vals))
        std = float(np.std(vals, ddof=1)) if len(vals) > 1 else 0.0
        print(f"[{args.method}] over {len(res)} seeds: Max-Cut value = {mean:.4f} ± {std:.4f}")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
