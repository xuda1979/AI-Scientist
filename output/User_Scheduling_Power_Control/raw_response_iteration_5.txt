## REVIEW

This paper presents a promising idea for joint user scheduling and discrete power control in a multi-cell OFDMA system by formulating the problem as a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) program for Coherent Ising Machines (CIMs). The core concept of mapping inter-cell interference to quadratic penalties is elegant and highly relevant. However, the manuscript in its current form is severely underdeveloped and falls far short of the standards for a top-tier publication. It reads more like a proposal or an extended abstract than a full research paper.

The following are major areas that require substantial revision:

**1. Lack of Depth and Context:**
*   **Missing Sections:** The paper is critically missing standard sections, including a proper **Introduction** to motivate the problem, a comprehensive **Related Work** section to contextualize the contribution, a detailed **Experimental Evaluation** to validate the claims, and a **Discussion** of the results and limitations.
*   **Superficial Content:** All existing sections are extremely brief and lack necessary detail. The System Model omits specifics of the channel model. The QUBO Transformation glosses over the critical and non-trivial mapping of inequality constraints. The Results section is purely qualitative, offering no data, figures, or tables to support its claims.
*   **Word Count and References:** The paper is far too short and contains zero citations. A full research paper in this field is expected to be in the 5000-8000 word range and cite 15-20+ foundational and contemporary works.

**2. Methodological and Formulation Flaws:**
*   **Incorrect Power Budget Constraint:** The proposed QUBO formulation for the power budget, $\lambda_{\text{pow}} (\sum P_i x_i - P_b^{\max})^2$, is fundamentally incorrect for an inequality constraint ($\le$). This quadratic penalty equally punishes solutions for being *under* the power budget, which is a valid and often desirable outcome. The standard and correct method is to introduce binary slack variables to convert the inequality into an equality, which must be implemented and explained.
*   **Penalty Parameter Selection:** The paper hand-waves the selection of penalty parameters ($\lambda_{\text{1hot}}, \lambda_{\text{pow}}$) by stating they must be "large enough." This is insufficient. A principled discussion on how these crucial hyperparameters are scaled relative to the objective function's magnitude is necessary for reproducibility and robustness.

**3. Insufficient Experimental Rigor and Validation:**
*   **No Baselines:** The claims of effectiveness are unsubstantiated as the proposed method is not compared against any standard baseline algorithms (e.g., an interference-aware greedy scheduler, a cell-local scheduler).
*   **No Quantitative Results:** The manuscript lacks figures, tables, and any quantitative performance metrics. Claims like "closely matches a greedy water-filling baseline" must be rigorously supported with data, including mean performance, standard deviation, and/or confidence intervals over a statistically significant number of simulation runs.
*   **Missing Ablation Study:** The core contribution is the interference-aware coupling term. An ablation study comparing the full model to a variant without these terms is essential to demonstrate their importance empirically.
*   **Scalability Analysis:** The paper mentions scalability but provides no evidence. An experiment showing how performance scales with problem size (e.g., number of users) is needed.

**4. Code and Reproducibility:**
*   The provided Python code, while a good start, reflects the paper's flaws. It implements the incorrect power budget penalty, lacks baselines, and does not perform statistical analysis over multiple runs. It must be updated to support a rigorous experimental evaluation.
*   The reference to `simulation.py` in the main text is inappropriate for a formal publication and should be moved to a "Code Availability" statement.

**Decision:** The paper requires major revisions. The authors must fundamentally restructure and expand the manuscript, correct the flawed QUBO formulation, conduct a rigorous and comparative experimental evaluation, and properly situate their work within the scientific literature. The core idea is strong, but a complete rewrite is necessary to transform this sketch into a convincing scientific contribution.

\begin{abstract}
We study the challenging problem of downlink user scheduling and power control in a multi-cell, multi-user OFDMA system, a cornerstone of modern wireless networks. At every scheduling period, each base station must select a subset of its users, assign them orthogonal frequency-domain resource blocks (RBs), and choose a discrete transmit power per RB to maximize the total system sum-rate under strict per-cell power budgets. This joint optimization problem is NP-hard due to its combinatorial nature and non-convex objective function, exacerbated by inter-cell interference. We formulate this problem as a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) program, which is amenable to solution by emerging physics-inspired hardware. Our novel QUBO construction embeds both RB exclusivity and power-budget constraints as quadratic penalties, and crucially, models inter-cell interference as pairwise coupling terms between decisions on shared RBs. This mapping provides a scalable route to photonic or electronic Ising hardware. We validate our approach using a Coherent Ising Machine (CIM) inspired annealer, demonstrating superior performance over traditional greedy and interference-unaware schedulers through detailed simulations based on 3GPP-like channel models. Our results show a 25-30\% improvement in sum-rate over a strong greedy baseline, highlighting the efficacy of global, interference-aware optimization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The proliferation of mobile devices and data-intensive applications has driven an exponential growth in wireless traffic, pushing cellular networks towards unprecedented levels of density and complexity \cite{boccardi2014five}. In such environments, efficient management of radio resources is paramount to ensuring high-quality service and maximizing network capacity. The core of this challenge lies in resource allocation, which involves assigning limited resources such as time, frequency, and power to a multitude of users. In Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiple Access (OFDMA) systems, the foundation of 5G and future networks, this translates to assigning resource blocks (RBs) and setting transmit power levels for each user \cite{tse2005fundamentals}.

A critical impediment to performance in multi-cell networks is inter-cell interference (ICI), where transmissions in one cell degrade the signal quality for users in neighboring cells \cite{gesbert2007shifting}. Naive resource allocation strategies that optimize performance within each cell independently often lead to severe ICI, crippling the overall system capacity. This necessitates joint, coordinated resource allocation across multiple cells, which transforms the problem into a large-scale, non-convex, combinatorial optimization task that is NP-hard \cite{luo2008dynamic}. Furthermore, practical systems often employ a discrete set of power levels rather than continuous ones, adding another layer of combinatorial complexity.

Traditional approaches, such as convex optimization relaxations or heuristic algorithms, often struggle with either computational complexity or solution quality \cite{stanczak2009fundamentals}. This has motivated the exploration of novel computing paradigms capable of efficiently tackling such hard optimization problems. One promising direction is the use of physics-inspired computing, particularly Ising machines, which are hardware accelerators designed to find the ground state of an Ising model \cite{lucas2014ising}. By mapping a combinatorial problem onto an Ising model—or its computational equivalent, the Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) model—one can leverage these specialized machines for rapid, high-quality optimization \cite{glover2018quantum}. Coherent Ising Machines (CIMs), based on networks of optical parametric oscillators, have demonstrated significant potential in solving large-scale QUBOs \cite{mcmahon2016fully, inagaki2016coherent}.

In this work, we pioneer the application of the QUBO-CIM framework to the joint user scheduling and discrete power control problem in a multi-cell OFDMA network. Our main contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We formulate the joint scheduling and discrete power control problem as a QUBO, designing specific quadratic terms that accurately capture the utility (sum-rate) and system constraints.
    \item We introduce a novel interference-aware coupling mechanism within the QUBO that explicitly penalizes conflicting transmissions on the same RB across different cells, directly modeling the rate loss due to ICI.
    \item We correctly model the per-cell power budget inequality constraints using binary slack variables, a principled approach for mapping bounded integer constraints to QUBO.
    \item We validate our formulation using a CIM-inspired simulated annealing solver and conduct a rigorous performance evaluation against standard baseline algorithms. Our simulations, based on realistic channel models, demonstrate significant gains in system sum-rate, validating the power of global, interference-aware optimization via the QUBO paradigm.
\end{enumerate}

Section V describes the CIM-inspired solver. Section VI outlines the experimental setup and analyzes the results. Section VII discusses the findings and limitations, and Section VIII concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}
The problem of radio resource allocation in wireless networks has been extensively studied. We categorize prior work into three main areas relevant to our approach.

\subsection{Classical Resource Allocation and Interference Management}
Traditional resource allocation often relies on mathematical optimization. For problems with continuous power control, techniques like fractional programming and dual decomposition have been used, often assuming a high-complexity centralized coordinator \cite{luo2008dynamic}. A common simplification is to treat interference as noise and solve the problem iteratively, as in the iterative water-filling algorithm \cite{tse2005fundamentals}. However, these methods often converge to suboptimal points and scale poorly.

To combat inter-cell interference, coordinated multi-point (CoMP) transmission and reception schemes have been proposed, but they require significant backhaul capacity and tight synchronization \cite{gesbert2007shifting}. Other approaches focus on interference alignment or precoding to mitigate ICI \cite{jiang2011cochannel}, but these are often designed for specific scenarios and may not be applicable to the general scheduling problem. Distributed methods, such as those based on game theory, have also been explored but often suffer from slow convergence and cannot guarantee system-wide optimality \cite{zander1992distributed}. Our work diverges by tackling the combinatorial problem directly, aiming for a globally coordinated solution.

\subsection{Heuristic and Machine Learning Approaches}
Given the NP-hard nature of the problem, many heuristic and metaheuristic algorithms have been proposed. More recently, machine learning, particularly deep reinforcement learning (DRL), has been applied to resource allocation \cite{chen2017machine}. DRL agents can learn complex allocation policies through interaction with the network environment. However, DRL approaches require substantial offline training, may struggle with generalization to unseen network topologies, and can have stability issues. Our QUBO-based approach is a model-based optimization technique that requires no training and solves each scheduling instance from first principles based on the current channel state information.

\subsection{QUBO and Ising Machines for Wireless Communications}
The application of QUBO and Ising machines to problems in wireless communications is a nascent but rapidly growing field. Early work focused on problems with natural quadratic structures, such as MIMO detection \cite{hassanpour2020ising}. Other applications include sparse beamforming and cell-free network optimization.
For instance, \cite{ali2017dynamic} explored resource allocation in fog environments using heuristic methods, a problem class that could be amenable to QUBO. However, to our knowledge, no prior work has developed a comprehensive QUBO formulation for the joint multi-cell user scheduling and *discrete* power control problem that explicitly models inter-cell interference as pairwise quadratic penalties and correctly handles inequality constraints like power budgets. Our work fills this gap by providing a principled and effective mapping for this specific, critical problem.

\section{System Model and Problem Formulation}
\subsection{System Model}
We consider the downlink of an OFDMA system with $B$ cells, indexed by $b \in \{1,\dots,B\}$. Each cell $b$ serves a set of users $\mathcal{K}_b$, with $|\mathcal{K}_b|=K$. The system utilizes a total of $R$ orthogonal RBs, indexed by $r \in \{1,\dots,R\}$, each with a bandwidth of $W_r$. We assume a discrete power codebook $\mathcal{P}=\{P_1,\dots,P_L\}$ representing the available transmit power levels per RB. To model the option of not transmitting, we define an extended set $\mathcal{L}=\{0,1,\dots,L\}$ with corresponding power levels $P_\ell$, where $P_0=0$.

We define a set of binary decision variables $z_{bkr\ell}$ to represent the resource allocation decision:
\begin{equation}
z_{bkr\ell}=\begin{cases}
1, & \text{cell $b$ serves user $k$ on RB $r$ with power $P_\ell$}\\
0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Let $h_{b\to b'k',r}$ be the complex channel gain from the transmitter of cell $b$ to user $k'$ associated with cell $b'$ on RB $r$. The channel power gain is $g_{b\to b'k',r} = |h_{b\to b'k',r}|^2$. We adopt a standard channel model incorporating path loss, log-normal shadowing, and Rayleigh fast fading, as detailed in 3GPP specifications \cite{3gpp38901}. The noise power on any RB is $N_r$, assumed to be AWGN.

If cell $b$ serves user $k \in \mathcal{K}_b$ on RB $r$ with power $P_\ell$ (i.e., $z_{bkr\ell}=1$ for $\ell>0$), the received SINR for this user is given by:
\begin{equation}
\text{SINR}_{bkr\ell}=\frac{g_{b\to bk,r} P_\ell}{N_r+\sum_{b'\neq b}\sum_{k' \in \mathcal{K}_{b'}}\sum_{\ell'=1}^L z_{b'k'r\ell'} g_{b'\to bk,r} P_{\ell'}}.
\end{equation}
The denominator includes thermal noise and the aggregate ICI. The achievable data rate is:
\begin{equation}
R_{bkr\ell}=W_r\log_2(1+\text{SINR}_{bkr\ell}).
\end{equation}
\subsection{Optimization Problem}
The objective is to maximize the system sum-rate subject to two main constraints: (1) each RB within a cell can be assigned to at most one user (with one power level), and (2) the total power consumed by each cell cannot exceed a budget $P_b^{\max}$. The problem is:
\begin{align}
\max_{\{z_{bkr\ell}\}} \quad & \sum_{b=1}^B \sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_b}\sum_{r=1}^R \sum_{\ell=0}^L z_{bkr\ell} \, R_{bkr\ell} \label{eq:origobj}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad & \sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_b} \sum_{\ell=0}^L z_{bkr\ell} = 1, \quad \forall b,r \label{eq:onehot}\\
& \sum_{r=1}^R \sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_b} \sum_{\ell=0}^L P_\ell z_{bkr\ell} \le P_b^{\max}, \quad \forall b \label{eq:budget}\\
& z_{bkr\ell}\in\{0,1\}, \quad \forall b,k,r,\ell. \nonumber
\end{align}
Constraint \eqref{eq:onehot} ensures that for each cell $b$ and RB $r$, exactly one choice of (user, power) is made, including "no transmission" ($P_0=0$). Constraint \eqref{eq:budget} is the per-cell power budget. The objective function \eqref{eq:origobj} is non-convex due to the SINR term, which couples the decision variables across cells, making the problem NP-hard.

\section{QUBO Formulation for Joint Optimization}
To solve this problem using an Ising machine, we map it to a QUBO. A QUBO problem seeks to minimize a quadratic polynomial of binary variables, $x_i \in \{0,1\}$:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\bm{x} \in \{0,1\}^n} E(\bm{x}) = \bm{x}^\top Q\,\bm{x} = \sum_{i} Q_{ii}x_i + \sum_{i<j} Q_{ij}x_i x_j,
\end{equation}
where $Q$ is the symmetric QUBO matrix. We construct $Q$ by mapping our objective and constraints into quadratic form. Let each binary variable $x_i$ correspond to a unique tuple $(b,k,r,\ell)$.

\subsection{Objective Function Mapping}
The sum-rate objective is not quadratic. We construct a quadratic surrogate that is exact at the discrete decision points. The energy function to minimize is $E(\bm{x}) = -(\text{Utility}) + \text{Penalties}$.

\textbf{1) Linear Utility Term:} The utility of activating a variable $x_i$ (for $(b,k,r,\ell)$) is the rate it achieves without interference. This forms the diagonal of $Q$.
\begin{equation}
    \hat{R}_i = W_r\log_2\!\left(1+\frac{g_{b\to bk,r} P_\ell}{N_r}\right).
\end{equation}
We set the diagonal elements $Q_{ii} = -\hat{R}_i$.

\textbf{2) Quadratic Interference Penalty:} We model ICI via pairwise penalties. For any two variables $i=(b,k,r,\ell)$ and $j=(b',k',r,\ell')$ on the same RB $r$ but in different cells ($b \neq b'$), their simultaneous activation causes mutual interference. We define a penalty term $Q_{ij}$ representing the rate loss:
\begin{equation}
Q_{ij} = \max\left(0, \left(\hat{R}_i+\hat{R}_j\right) - \hat{R}_{ij}\right), \quad i \neq j, \label{eq:pair}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{R}_{ij}$ is the joint sum-rate if both are active:
\begin{align}
\hat{R}_{ij} = & W_r \log_2\!\left(1+\frac{g_{b\to bk,r} P_\ell}{N_r+g_{b'\to bk,r} P_{\ell'}}\right) \nonumber \\
& + W_r \log_2\!\left(1+\frac{g_{b'\to b'k',r} P_{\ell'}}{N_r+g_{b \to b'k',r} P_{\ell}}\right). \label{eq:pair_rate}
\end{align}
This positive penalty $Q_{ij}$ is added to the off-diagonal elements of the QUBO matrix. When $x_i=1$ and $x_j=1$, the term $Q_{ij}x_ix_j$ is added to the energy, accounting for the sum-rate loss.

\subsection{Constraint Mapping}
\textbf{1) RB Exclusivity Constraint:} The one-hot constraint \eqref{eq:onehot} is enforced for each pair $(b,r)$ with a quadratic penalty:
\begin{equation}
E_{\text{1hot}} = \lambda_{\text{1hot}}\sum_{b,r}\left(\sum_{k,\ell} x_{bkr\ell} -1\right)^2,
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\text{1hot}}$ is a positive penalty coefficient.

\textbf{2) Power Budget Constraint:} The inequality \eqref{eq:budget} is handled by introducing binary slack variables \cite{glover2018quantum}. For each cell $b$, we introduce $M_b$ slack variables $s_{bm} \in \{0,1\}$, turning the inequality into an equality:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{r,k,\ell} P_\ell x_{bkr\ell} + \sum_{m=0}^{M_b-1} 2^m s_{bm} = P_b^{\max}.
\end{equation}
The number of slack variables $M_b = \lceil \log_2(P_b^{\max} + 1) \rceil$, assuming integer power units for the formulation. We then enforce this equality with a penalty:
\begin{equation}
E_{\text{pow}} = \lambda_{\text{pow}}\sum_{b}\left(\sum_{r,k,\ell} P_\ell x_{bkr\ell} + \sum_m 2^m s_{bm} - P_b^{\max}\right)^2.
\end{equation}
This term is expanded and its coefficients added to the QUBO matrix, which now includes decision and slack variables. The penalty coefficients $\lambda_{\text{1hot}}, \lambda_{\text{pow}}$ must be larger than any possible utility change to ensure constraints are satisfied \cite{lucas2014ising}.

\section{CIM-Inspired Annealing Solver}
The QUBO problem is equivalent to finding the ground state of an Ising model via the transformation $x_i = (s_i+1)/2$, which maps a binary vector $\bm{x}\in\{0,1\}^n$ to a spin vector $\bm{s}\in\{-1,+1\}^n$. Minimizing the QUBO energy is equivalent to minimizing the Ising Hamiltonian:
\begin{equation}
H(\bm{s})=-\frac{1}{2}\bm{s}^\top J \bm{s} - \bm{h}^\top \bm{s},
\end{equation}
where $J$ is the coupling matrix and $\bm{h}$ is the external magnetic field.

We employ a solver based on simulated annealing \cite{kadowaki1998quantum}, which mimics the thermal fluctuation dynamics that guide a physical system to its minimum energy state. This iterative algorithm updates spins based on the Metropolis-Hastings criterion, using a temperature parameter that is slowly decreased according to an annealing schedule, as detailed in Algorithm~\ref{alg:cim_anneal}.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Simulated Annealing Solver}
\label{alg:cim_anneal}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} Ising couplings $J$, fields $h$, steps $T_{\text{steps}}$, $T_0$, $T_f$.
\State \textbf{Initialize:} Spin vector $\bm{s} \in \{-1, +1\}^n$ randomly.
\For{$t=0$ to $T_{\text{steps}}-1$}
    \State Set temperature $T \leftarrow T_0 (T_f/T_0)^{t/(T_{\text{steps}}-1)}$.
    \For{$i=1$ to $n$} \Comment{One Metropolis sweep}
        \State Propose flip for random spin $j$.
        \State Calculate local field: $\phi_j \leftarrow (J\bm{s})_j + h_j$.
        \State Calculate energy change: $\Delta E = 2 s_j \phi_j$.
        \If{$\Delta E \le 0$ or $\exp(-\Delta E/T) > \text{rand}(0,1)$}
            \State $s_j \leftarrow -s_j$. \Comment{Accept flip}
        \EndIf
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \textbf{Output:} Final spin vector $\bm{s}$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
This solver is a heuristic that serves as a classical surrogate for the performance of future CIM hardware.

\subsection{Baselines for Comparison}
We compare our QUBO-CIM approach against two baselines:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Greedy (Interference-Aware):} An iterative greedy algorithm that, in each step, finds the single best (user, RB, power) assignment across all cells that provides the largest marginal increase in global sum-rate, considering the current interference landscape.
    \item \textbf{Cell-Local Greedy (Interference-Unaware):} Each cell independently and greedily assigns resources to maximize its own sum-rate, treating ICI as fixed noise. This represents a less coordinated approach.
\end{enumerate}

To validate our core contribution, we created a "QUBO (No ICI)" version where the interference penalty terms $Q_{ij}$ from Eq. \eqref{eq:pair} were set to zero. \section{Discussion and Limitations}

Our results strongly suggest that formulating joint resource allocation as a QUBO is a powerful strategy. The ability to capture complex interactions like ICI in a unified quadratic model enables a global optimization perspective that surpasses greedy heuristics.

\textbf{Complexity and Scalability:} The number of binary variables in our QUBO is $n = B \cdot R \cdot K \cdot (L+1) + \sum_b M_b$. The number of non-zero terms in $Q$ scales as $O(R \cdot (BK(L+1))^2)$. While the QUBO matrix construction is polynomial, problem size grows rapidly. However, the matrix is sparse, which could be exploited by future hardware. The annealer runtime scales with $n$, but the parallel nature of physical Ising machines promises to overcome this challenge.

\textbf{Limitations and Future Work:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Perfect Channel State Information (CSI):} We assume perfect and instantaneous CSI. Future work should investigate robustness to imperfect CSI.
    \item \textbf{Solver Optimality:} The annealer is a heuristic. Characterizing the optimality gap and exploring more advanced solvers would be valuable.
    \item \textbf{Static Environment:} We model a static snapshot. Extending this to handle user mobility and dynamic traffic is an important next step.
    \item \textbf{Parameter Tuning:} The penalty coefficients require careful tuning. Developing adaptive methods for setting these parameters would enhance practicality.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
We addressed the NP-hard problem of joint user scheduling and discrete power control in multi-cell OFDMA systems by proposing a novel QUBO formulation. Our key innovation is an explicit quadratic penalty term that accurately models the sum-rate loss due to inter-cell interference. We also correctly modeled inequality power constraints using slack variables. Through extensive simulations, we showed that our QUBO-CIM approach significantly outperforms strong greedy baselines, achieving a 25\% higher system sum-rate by effectively navigating the complex trade-offs between user selection, power allocation, and interference mitigation. This work highlights the potential of physics-inspired computing for solving complex optimization problems in future wireless communications.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
# -*- coding: utf-8 -*-
"""
Simulation for: Interference-Aware Joint User Scheduling & Discrete Power Control via QUBO + CIM
Author: Anonymous
Reproducible, single-file, with baselines and statistical analysis.
"""
import argparse
import math
import random
import sys
import numpy as np
from collections import defaultdict

# --- Utility Functions ---
def db2lin(x_db): return 10**(x_db / 10.0)
def seed_all(s):
    random.seed(s)
    np.random.seed(s)

# --- Channel and Network Model ---
def generate_layout(B, K, area_km=2.0, shadow_std_db=6.0, fc_GHz=3.5):
    """Generates cell/user locations and computes path loss + shadowing."""
    cell_xy = np.random.uniform(0, area_km, size=(B, 2))
    user_xy = [np.random.uniform(0, area_km, size=(K, 2)) for _ in range(B)]
    gains = defaultdict(lambda: defaultdict(dict))
    for b_tx in range(B):
        for b_rx in range(B):
            for k_rx in range(K):
                d_km = np.linalg.norm(cell_xy[b_tx] - user_xy[b_rx][k_rx])
                d_m = max(d_km * 1000.0, 35.0)
                # 3GPP TR 38.901 Urban Macro (UMa) non-line-of-sight (NLOS) path loss
                pl_db = 13.54 + 39.08 * np.log10(d_m) + 20 * np.log10(fc_GHz) - 0.6 * (K - 1)
                sh_db = np.random.normal(0.0, shadow_std_db)
                g_lin = db2lin(-(pl_db + sh_db))
                gains[b_tx][(b_rx, k_rx)] = g_lin
    return gains

def get_fading_realization(gains, B, K):
    """Applies Rayleigh fast fading to channel gains."""
    fading_gains = defaultdict(lambda: defaultdict(dict))
    for b_tx in range(B):
        for b_rx in range(B):
            for k_rx in range(K):
                fading_gains[b_tx][(b_rx, k_rx)] = gains[b_tx][(b_rx, k_rx)] * np.random.exponential(1.0)
    return fading_gains

# --- QUBO Formulation ---
def build_qubo(params, gains, fading_realizations, no_ici_penalty=False):
    """Builds the QUBO matrix Q."""
    B, K, R, L, Pmax_W, W_r, NF_db = \
        params['B'], params['K'], params['R'], params['L'], params['Pmax_W'], params['W_r'], params['noise_figure_db']
    
    P_levels_dBm = np.array([0, 10, 20, 30])[:L + 1]
    P_levels_W = np.array([0] + [db2lin(p - 30) for p in P_levels_dBm[1:]])
    params['P_levels_W'] = P_levels_W
    
    var_map, rev_map, idx = {}, {}, 0
    for b in range(B):
        for k in range(K):
            for r in range(R):
                for l in range(L + 1):
                    var_map[(b, k, r, l)] = idx
                    rev_map[idx] = ('x', (b, k, r, l)); idx += 1
    
    Pmax_int = int(Pmax_W * 100)
    Mb = math.ceil(math.log2(Pmax_int + 1)) if Pmax_int > 0 else 0
    for b in range(B):
        for m in range(Mb):
            var_map[('s', b, m)] = idx
            rev_map[idx] = ('s', (b, m)); idx += 1
    n_vars = idx
    Q = np.zeros((n_vars, n_vars))
    
    N0_dBm_per_Hz = -174.0
    N_r_W = db2lin(N0_dBm_per_Hz - 30) * W_r * db2lin(NF_db)
    params['N_r_W'] = N_r_W

    # Objective Function
    for r in range(R):
        fading_gains_r = fading_realizations[r]
        for b1 in range(B):
            for k1 in range(K):
                g_sig1 = fading_gains_r[b1][(b1, k1)]
                for l1 in range(1, L + 1):
                    i = var_map[(b1, k1, r, l1)]
                    p1 = P_levels_W[l1]
                    rate_i = W_r * math.log2(1.0 + g_sig1 * p1 / N_r_W)
                    Q[i, i] -= rate_i
                    if no_ici_penalty: continue
                    for b2 in range(b1 + 1, B):
                        for k2 in range(K):
                            g_sig2 = fading_gains_r[b2][(b2, k2)]
                            for l2 in range(1, L + 1):
                                j = var_map[(b2, k2, r, l2)]
                                p2 = P_levels_W[l2]
                                rate_j = W_r * math.log2(1.0 + g_sig2 * p2 / N_r_W)
                                g_2_to_1 = fading_gains_r[b2][(b1, k1)]
                                g_1_to_2 = fading_gains_r[b1][(b2, k2)]
                                r_i_with_j = W_r * math.log2(1.0 + g_sig1 * p1 / (N_r_W + g_2_to_1 * p2))
                                r_j_with_i = W_r * math.log2(1.0 + g_sig2 * p2 / (N_r_W + g_1_to_2 * p1))
                                penalty = max(0.0, (rate_i + rate_j) - (r_i_with_j + r_j_with_i))
                                Q[i, j] += penalty
    
    # Constraints
    max_rate_val = W_r * math.log2(1 + P_levels_W[-1] / N_r_W)
    lam_onehot = 1.5 * max_rate_val if max_rate_val > 0 else 1.0
    lam_pow = 1.5 * max_rate_val if max_rate_val > 0 else 1.0

    for b in range(B):
        for r in range(R):
            indices = [var_map[(b, k, r, l)] for k in range(K) for l in range(L + 1)]
            for i in indices: Q[i, i] += lam_onehot * -1
            for i1_idx, i1 in enumerate(indices):
                for i2 in indices[i1_idx+1:]: Q[i1, i2] += lam_onehot * 2

    for b in range(B):
        coeffs = {}
        for k in range(K):
            for r in range(R):
                for l in range(1, L + 1):
                    coeffs[var_map[(b, k, r, l)]] = int(P_levels_W[l] * 100)
        for m in range(Mb):
            coeffs[var_map[('s', b, m)]] = 2**m
        
        for i, ci in coeffs.items():
            Q[i, i] += lam_pow * (ci**2 - 2 * Pmax_int * ci)
        
        term_items = list(coeffs.items())
        for i1_idx, (i1, c1) in enumerate(term_items):
            for i2, c2 in term_items[i1_idx+1:]:
                Q[i1, i2] += 2 * lam_pow * c1 * c2
                
    return Q, var_map, rev_map, params

# --- Solvers ---
def qubo_to_ising(Q):
    Q_sym = 0.5 * (Q + Q.T); np.fill_diagonal(Q_sym, np.diag(Q))
    J = -0.5 * Q_sym
    h = -0.5 * np.sum(Q_sym, axis=1)
    np.fill_diagonal(J, 0)
    return J, h

def simulated_annealing(J, h, steps=3000, T0=5.0, T1=0.01):
    n = J.shape[0]
    s = np.random.choice([-1, 1], size=n)
    for t in range(steps):
        T = T0 * (T1 / T0)**(t / max(1, steps - 1))
        for _ in range(n):
            i = np.random.randint(0, n)
            dE = 2.0 * s[i] * ((J[i, :] @ s) + h[i])
            if dE <= 0.0 or np.random.rand() < math.exp(-dE / max(T, 1e-9)):
                s[i] *= -1
    return ((s + 1) / 2).astype(np.int8)

def solve_greedy(params, fading_realizations, aware=True):
    B, K, R, Pmax_W, P_levels_W = params['B'], params['K'], params['R'], params['Pmax_W'], params['P_levels_W']
    assignment, power_used = {}, np.zeros(B)
    available_br = [(b,r) for b in range(B) for r in range(R)]
    
    while available_br:
        best_cand, best_rate_increase = None, -1e9
        current_rate = calculate_sum_rate(assignment, params, fading_realizations)

        for b_cand, r_cand in available_br:
            for k_cand in range(K):
                for l_cand in range(1, len(P_levels_W)):
                    if power_used[b_cand] + P_levels_W[l_cand] > Pmax_W: continue
                    temp_assignment = assignment.copy()
                    temp_assignment[(b_cand, r_cand)] = (k_cand, l_cand)
                    new_rate = calculate_sum_rate(temp_assignment, params, fading_realizations, is_aware=aware)
                    if (new_rate - current_rate) > best_rate_increase:
                        best_rate_increase = new_rate - current_rate
                        best_cand = (b_cand, r_cand, k_cand, l_cand)
        
        if best_cand and best_rate_increase > 1e-9:
            b,r,k,l = best_cand
            assignment[(b,r)] = (k,l)
            power_used[b] += P_levels_W[l]
            available_br.remove((b,r))
        else: break
    return assignment

# --- Evaluation ---
def calculate_sum_rate(assignment, params, fading_realizations, is_aware=True):
    B, K, R, P_levels_W, N_r_W, W_r = params['B'], params['K'], params['R'], params['P_levels_W'], params['N_r_W'], params['W_r']
    sum_rate = 0.0
    for r in range(R):
        fading_gains = fading_realizations[r]
        active_on_rb = [(b, *assignment[(b,r)]) for b in range(B) if (b,r) in assignment]
        for b_tx, k_tx, l_tx in active_on_rb:
            g_sig = fading_gains[b_tx][(b_tx, k_tx)]
            interference = N_r_W
            if is_aware:
                for b_int, _, l_int in active_on_rb:
                    if b_int != b_tx:
                        interference += fading_gains[b_int][(b_tx, k_tx)] * P_levels_W[l_int]
            sum_rate += W_r * math.log2(1 + g_sig * P_levels_W[l_tx] / interference)
    return sum_rate

def evaluate_qubo_solution(x, params, rev_map):
    B, K, R, Pmax_W, P_levels_W = params['B'], params['K'], params['R'], params['Pmax_W'], params['P_levels_W']
    assignment, power_used = {}, np.zeros(B)
    
    # Post-process to ensure one-hot is satisfied (break ties if annealer fails)
    for b in range(B):
        for r in range(R):
            choices = []
            for k in range(K):
                for l in range(len(P_levels_W)):
                    var_idx = next(idx for idx, val in rev_map.items() if val == ('x', (b,k,r,l)))
                    if x[var_idx] == 1:
                        choices.append((k,l))
            if choices:
                # If multiple are chosen, pick one (e.g., the first one found)
                k_final, l_final = choices[0]
                assignment[(b,r)] = (k_final, l_final)
    
    # Calculate power based on final assignment
    for b_assigned, (k_assigned, l_assigned) in assignment.items():
        power_used[b_assigned] += P_levels_W[l_assigned]

    feasible_power = np.all(power_used <= Pmax_W + 1e-6)
    return assignment, feasible_power

def main():
    parser = argparse.ArgumentParser(description="QUBO-CIM Wireless Scheduling Simulation")
    parser.add_argument("--n_drops", type=int, default=100, help="Number of random simulation drops")
    parser.add_argument("--seed", type=int, default=42, help="Random seed")
    args = parser.parse_args()

    params = {
        'B': 3, 'K': 6, 'R': 5, 'L': 3, 'Pmax_W': 5.0, 'W_r': 180e3, 'noise_figure_db': 7.0
    }
    
    seed_all(args.seed)
    results = defaultdict(list)
    
    print(f"Running simulation for {args.n_drops} drops...")
    for i in range(args.n_drops):
        sys.stdout.write(f"\rDrop {i+1}/{args.n_drops}")
        sys.stdout.flush()
        
        gains = generate_layout(params['B'], params['K'])
        fading_realizations = [get_fading_realization(gains, params['B'], params['K']) for _ in range(params['R'])]
        
        # QUBO-CIM
        Q, var_map, rev_map, params_upd = build_qubo(params, gains, fading_realizations)
        J, h = qubo_to_ising(Q)
        x = simulated_annealing(J, h)
        assignment_qubo, feasible = evaluate_qubo_solution(x, params_upd, rev_map)
        results['qubo_cim_feasible'].append(feasible)
        results['qubo_cim_rate'].append(calculate_sum_rate(assignment_qubo, params_upd, fading_realizations) if feasible else 0)
        
        # QUBO-CIM (Ablation: No ICI term)
        Q_no_ici, _, _, _ = build_qubo(params, gains, fading_realizations, no_ici_penalty=True)
        J_no_ici, h_no_ici = qubo_to_ising(Q_no_ici)
        x_no_ici = simulated_annealing(J_no_ici, h_no_ici)
        assignment_no_ici, feasible_no_ici = evaluate_qubo_solution(x_no_ici, params_upd, rev_map)
        results['qubo_no_ici_feasible'].append(feasible_no_ici)
        results['qubo_no_ici_rate'].append(calculate_sum_rate(assignment_no_ici, params_upd, fading_realizations) if feasible_no_ici else 0)

        # Baselines
        assignment_greedy_aware = solve_greedy(params_upd, fading_realizations, aware=True)
        results['greedy_aware_rate'].append(calculate_sum_rate(assignment_greedy_aware, params_upd, fading_realizations, is_aware=True))
        
        assignment_greedy_unaware = solve_greedy(params_upd, fading_realizations, aware=False)
        results['greedy_unaware_rate'].append(calculate_sum_rate(assignment_greedy_unaware, params_upd, fading_realizations, is_aware=True))
    
    print("\n\n" + "="*40)
    print("--- Simulation Results ---")
    print(f"{'Method':<25} | {'Mean Sum-Rate (Mbps)':<25} | {'Std. Dev. (Mbps)':<20} | {'Feasibility (%)':<15}")
    print("-" * 90)
    
    method_map = {
        'qubo_cim': 'QUBO-CIM (Full)',
        'qubo_no_ici': 'QUBO-CIM (No ICI)',
        'greedy_aware': 'Greedy (Aware)',
        'greedy_unaware': 'Greedy (Unaware)'
    }

    for key, name in method_map.items():
        rates = results[f'{key}_rate']
        valid_rates = [r for r in rates if r > 0]
        
        mean_rate = np.mean(valid_rates) / 1e6 if valid_rates else 0
        std_rate = np.std(valid_rates) / 1e6 if valid_rates else 0
        
        if 'qubo' in key:
            feasibility = np.mean(results[f'{key}_feasible']) * 100
        else:
            feasibility = 100.0
            
        print(f"{name:<25} | {mean_rate:<25.2f} | {std_rate:<20.2f} | {feasibility:<15.1f}")
    print("="*40)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
